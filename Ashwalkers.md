# LYRIA
## Wasteland Lore Compendium
### The Ashwalkers: Veth'Karaal

---

## Overview

The Ashwalkers are what two thousand years of the Wastelands made. They are a Lyrian subspecies shaped entirely by the most hostile environment on an already dying planet, and they live in the ruins of what was once their civilization's greatest city. What they have built there sits somewhere between civilization and ruin and works on its own terms. Nothing about them is fragile. They are the people hard enough to stay when everyone else left.

They have no single name for themselves, because they have never been unified long enough for one to stick. Veth'Karaal, a corrupted derivation of old Elyric meaning roughly *the burning dead*, is what the Moonweavers called them, and it has spread far enough across the galaxy that most outsiders use it first. Among themselves, clan comes first and the Wastelands second. They acknowledge the broader category when dealing with outsiders and ignore it otherwise.

## Society

### Clan Structure

A clan forms around a dominant individual whose debt-holdings, fighters, and access to the ruins make alliance more survivable than opposition. Obligation matters more than blood. Even so, because the fertility crisis makes viable lineages so valuable, clans record bloodlines carefully, though the wider culture treats most history as irrelevant.

Clans grow when a dominant figure absorbs smaller groups and can't be dislodged. They shatter when that figure falls and their debts fail to transfer cleanly, which is almost always, since the qualities that make someone dominant in the Wastelands rarely include training a successor. Wasteland history is a run of near-unifications, each remembered in oral tradition as both glory and warning. Anyone powerful enough to pull everything together becomes, by that same power, someone every smaller player needs removed at once. The Moonweavers and the Suneaters have long understood this, and neither wants to see it change. Their interference is quiet, deniable, and steady across centuries, and some Ashwalkers have begun to notice the pattern even if they can't prove it.

### Men and Women

At its public level, to whatever degree the Wastelands have levels, Ashwalker society is male-dominated. In the early generations physical survival was the only currency, and the selection pressure of that time hardened into a cultural expectation that has never shifted. Male authority is the default assumption.

Underneath, things are more complicated. Women in the Wastelands have to be formidable, and the fertility crisis gives their ability to bear children a weight that cultural dominance can't fully cancel. Men can't manufacture it. The most politically capable women know exactly what it is worth and move it through debt networks without announcing what they are doing. Who owes reproductive access to whom, and what that access is worth at the moment, runs as a quiet calculation beneath most significant clan negotiations. The women holding those threads rarely hold formal authority, since formal authority depends on a kind of performance designed to shut them out. What they hold tends to last longer. It can't be taken by force and has to be dismantled piece by piece, and anyone who does that openly shows everyone watching what they are afraid of.

### The Delver

Every clan of consequence has a Delver, someone who knows the ruins as well as their own hands. They know the structural language of old Lyrian architecture and which sections have held for two thousand years and which will collapse without warning. They know where pre-Cataclysm technology concentrates, and they can tell the ordinary silence of dead stone from the silence that means something corrupted is close. None of this is written down, because a Delver who writes it down becomes replaceable and gives up their only leverage. It passes through apprenticeship instead, over years spent following a master into places that would kill an untrained person, learning through proximity, repetition, and the occasional near-death that teaches faster than anything else. Sons and daughters inherit the role most often. Blood is the closest thing to trust that works in the Wastelands, so the knowledge stays in the family.

A Delver is the most valuable member of any clan and, in every practical sense, its prisoner. They eat first. Their salvage claims are inviolable, even by clan leadership, and no leader with any sense makes a public enemy of their Delver. The confinement is just as firm. A Delver may not get lost or take unnecessary risks, and when they go deep, an escort goes with them, protective in name and a leash in practice. Every comfort they receive reminds them of what they can't be allowed to do freely.

For a woman, the role offers something rarer than status. Her value as a Delver can't be reduced to childbearing, and the clan's need for what she knows gives her leverage outside the usual calculations made about women. She is still confined, but by her own indispensability instead of by ownership. Some women pursue the role for exactly that reason. The ruins are dangerous, and so is everything else on offer.

### Pregnancy and Death

Ashwalkers treat pregnancy as the critical resource event it is. A pregnant woman is protected fiercely and without sentiment. She isn't worked as hard, isn't put in the same danger, and isn't treated as expendable the way most Ashwalkers are most of the time. That protection comes with possessiveness, and both are sincere.

A woman who has carried a healthy child to term holds a different position in her clan afterward. The pregnancy proved she could conceive and carry, the child's survival proved something more, and the people around her remember both. She gains no formal authority from it. She gains weight in everyone else's calculations about her, and in Ashwalker society that weight counts for a great deal.

Death is mainly a logistical problem. The body is dealt with quickly, since plenty of things in the Wastelands will come to investigate a corpse, and the more pressing question follows: what the dead person held. Debts owed to them don't transfer automatically. Their salvage claims go to whoever can hold them, meaning whoever in the clan is strong enough to enforce the claim and motivated enough to try. The scramble after a significant clan member dies is often more dangerous than whatever killed them.

### Debt, Servitude, and the Owned

Nobody had to invent indentured servitude in the Wastelands. It grew out of the debt system on its own. When a debt grows large enough and the debtor has nothing left but labor and compliance, the creditor collects that. The ledger doesn't care what form payment takes.

Owners don't treat indentured people carelessly, because damaging property is bad economics. They work, they are fed enough to keep working, and they carry the debt until it is paid or until they die, at which point it passes to their children. Outright slavery is simpler. Slaves are outsiders caught unprepared, prisoners from rival clans whose deaths would waste a resource, or people sold by clans that needed something to trade. The difference between slave and indentured servant matters far more to the person in that position than to the person holding them.

What keeps people trapped is that the owner decides how the debt accumulates, and disputing the arithmetic takes leverage most indentured people don't have. There is a way out. A skilled fighter, someone with knowledge the clan needs, or someone who has quietly built up small debt-holdings of their own over years of careful maneuvering can sometimes renegotiate their way to something like freedom. It is uncommon, but it happens often enough that the people at the bottom keep believing in it, and that belief does a good deal to keep the system stable.

### The Spoken Record

Ashwalkers keep almost no written records. Debt ledgers and bloodline records are the exceptions, too valuable to trust to memory and too dangerous to leave ambiguous. Everything else travels by word of mouth.

As a result, history in the Wastelands stays fluid. It bends toward whoever is telling it and whatever they need it to do. The story of how a clan was founded, what was owed at the start, who sacrificed what, and who took the credit shifts a little with every retelling, and more than a little when the teller has something riding on which version survives. Two clans can hold completely different accounts of the same event, and neither can prove the other wrong. Everyone uses that ambiguity, and they use it on purpose.

The people who control how stories are told hold a quiet kind of power outside the usual physical hierarchy. An elder too old to fight who holds the accepted version of something that happened two generations back still has influence. The past is the only evidence Ashwalkers have for which debts exist and who owes what to whom, so whoever remembers it gets to decide it.

### Outsiders

The Veth'Karaal have no tradition of xenophobia, because they have no tradition of turning down a possible resource. An outsider is assessed on arrival for what they carry and what they know, and for what they need and how much leverage that need gives the clan. Those who arrive with nothing useful and no good reason to be kept alive usually die to the Wastelands before the Ashwalkers have to decide anything. Those with something to offer get a closer look, and depending on how the arithmetic works out, they may end up anywhere between reluctant ally and indentured laborer. Few outsiders who enter Ashwalker territory leave with the status they arrived with.

Species that came through the Tears over the generations have added to the variety in modern Ashwalkers. It shows in unusual bone densities, senses with no clear Lyrian origin, and the different ways bioluminescence expresses from one body to the next. In their brutal way the Ashwalkers are the most integrated population on the planet. In a place that kills almost everything, you use whatever arrives.

## Bonds

### Tai'shan
#### The Hidden Bond

The Tai'shan doesn't ask permission. It takes hold as indifferently as the corruption in the ground, and the Ashwalker it happens to is left to deal with the consequences.

In a society where any visible attachment invites attack, the life-bond is among the most dangerous things that can happen to a person. Acknowledging it publicly gives every rival and creditor the most efficient leverage there is. The bond goes underground instead. It lives in stolen hours and in deep ruins where nobody follows, and it shows only in gestures small enough that outsiders miss them: a particular silence, or a body that places itself between someone and a doorway without drawing attention to it.

The bioluminescence makes the secret imperfect. A bonded Ashwalker near their mate glows differently, with a warmth the corruption's cold light doesn't usually carry. Those who know what to look for will see it, and most choose not to, because saying it aloud would create obligations nobody wants.

The bond gives something nothing else in the Wastelands can. The paranoia that hums beneath every waking moment goes quiet in a bonded mate's presence. These are people who have never known what it is like to be somewhere without cataloguing its dangers, and that relief is hard to describe. It is closer to the lifting of a weight so constant it had stopped registering as weight at all.

That is why bond-death undoes Ashwalkers so completely. The weight returns for good, with no prospect of relief.

Ashwalkers who have lived long enough recognize a bond in others when they see one. They don't comment on it or interfere. Some debts would cost the collector more than they are worth.

## Appearance

The Wastelands don't make gentle things, and Ashwalkers wear the evidence plainly. Their skin runs in deep, desaturated tones of charcoal, ash-brown, and pale gray, and the bioluminescence supplies the only vivid color most of them have. Ancestral lineage sometimes shows in the undertone, a warmth suggesting Suneater blood or a faint blue-gray from Moonweaver ancestry. Those with heavier lineage influence can run toward dark purples or ashy crimsons that stand out against the usual palette, traces of bloodlines that haven't fully dissolved into the gray. In heavily saturated individuals the skin turns faintly translucent, and the veins show at all times.

At rest the bioluminescence runs faint along the veins and nerve pathways beneath the skin, concentrated at the throat, inner wrists, temples, and horn lines. In heavily saturated individuals the patterns spread further, threading across the chest and down the arms in fractal shapes that mirror the broken ground outside. The color depends on lineage and accumulated exposure. Light saturation reads as greenish-white, and deeper corruption pulls it toward a colder, harder green. The most heavily touched pulse between green and a sickly, bruised gold. None of it is cosmetic, and none of it can be controlled. The glow dims in sleep and brightens with exertion, pain, and strong emotion. During the Ardor it surges into a full-body flare that leaves nothing to interpretation.

The bioluminescence is most visible in the eyes. It has largely overtaken natural iris color, washing it pale in lightly saturated individuals and ringing the pupil with a faint glow that responds to the body's state. In heavily saturated individuals the iris itself glows steadily, giving them a lamplike look in low light that most beings find very uncomfortable up close. Their vision has adapted to the dark, and strong direct light hurts.

Most Ashwalkers descend from the Moonweaver falling-horn pattern, but their horns are denser and more irregular, and they don't shed cleanly. Old layers compress and fracture outward into a rough, layered surface, cracked along its length, with bioluminescence showing at the fault lines as though the light were trying to get out. Ornamental modification is uncommon. Practical modification is not, and some individuals have attachments worked directly into the horn over years. Heavily saturated males in particular wear jewelry at the horn's fault lines and base, dark metals and corrupted crystal settings chosen to catch and reflect the glow. They do it from the same instinct behind the venom-mark, to put what is already there on display so that everyone in the room reads it correctly.

Between corruption's calcifying effect and the brutal physical selection of the environment, Ashwalkers have been pushed to the upper end of the Lyrian height range. Most men stand between 6'2" and 6'6", and heavily saturated individuals occasionally exceed that. They are heavier than they look, since corruption-thickened tissue is dense, and outsiders are often caught off guard by it in close physical contact. Women stand between 5'8" and 6'2" and carry the same density.

Radiation has stripped most of the pigment from their hair. White, pale gray, and washed silver are common, and a dim reddish-brown persists in those with heavier Suneater ancestry. Hair carries no cultural meaning. It gets cut when it becomes inconvenient and is otherwise ignored.

## Biology

### Bodies

Ashwalker bodies carry two thousand years of evidence. Along the shoulders, forearms, and spine, the skin has thickened past the Lyrian baseline into something almost mineral, where corruption has calcified beneath the dermis into dense, irregular nodules. Nobody designed it as armor. It is what happens when the soft parts die and the rest keeps going.

Their redundant systems have been pushed further than in any other Lyrian subspecies. The dual hearts that define their kind have developed an almost alarming synchronization in Ashwalkers, each compensating for the other so fast that trauma that would drop most beings barely registers as a pause. Luminari field surgeons who have had to work on them describe the experience with consistent unease. Ashwalkers don't stay down the way bodies are supposed to.

### Corruption Saturation

Saturation varies from one Ashwalker to the next. It builds over a lifetime of exposure and can't be reversed, only slowed by time spent away from active corruption zones. Managing it is a balancing act with no cure at the end of it. The body needs a baseline level of saturation to function. Too little and it begins to fail, the shortfall registering as a slow withdrawal that worsens over months in clean environments. Too much and the lifespan shortens, fertility collapses, and the body burns through itself. Territory near the Outlands border is worth fighting over because it sits at the edge of that range. It gives those approaching a dangerous level somewhere to ease off without triggering the withdrawal that fully clean air produces. For most Ashwalkers, saturation is a tide they manage.

The heavier the saturation, the denser and stronger the body becomes, and the more the Wastelands' background paranoia settles into something useful instead of crippling. It also shortens life, and at high enough levels it nearly ends fertility. The most physically dominant and visually striking Ashwalkers are burning through their time fastest, are least able to father children, and pay the heaviest price for the place they have mastered. In the Wastelands, power comes with an expiry date written on the skin.

### Fertility

Generations of radiation, corrupted Majik saturation, and chemical residue have degraded male fertility in the Wastelands. The more corrupted a man is, the less likely he is to father children at all. This sits at the center of Ashwalker society, whether anyone says so openly or not.

Viable males are tracked. In a culture that writes almost nothing down, paternity and bloodline viability are recorded, remembered, and fought over, because they are among the few things that can't be taken by force. The most genetically sound men are identified young, and that designation stays with them whatever else they become.

Heavily corrupted men are in a complicated position. Their fertility is reduced but rarely gone, and they make up for its unreliability through claim. A powerful man doesn't negotiate access. He asserts it, and over time that assertion tends to produce results whatever the odds. When status makes refusal difficult, sheer frequency becomes a strategy. A child fathered by a heavily corrupted man carries the lineage of someone who survived what should have consumed him, and the culture gives that weight.

Men with lower saturation are the most fertile, which inverts every other status marker in Ashwalker society. They are lighter, less imposing, and carry less of the corruption that signals power and survival, yet the fertility crisis makes them more valuable than their standing suggests. The most powerful men in a clan deal with that threat to their position by claiming those men outright instead of competing with them. The venom-mark makes the claim visible to everyone present. Sexual relationships between the two are common and serve the same purpose as the mark, establishing possession in terms the culture recognizes. Whether anything more complicated grows beneath that is up to the individuals involved.

Women pay their own price to the environment. Pregnancy is dangerous, infant mortality is a constant, and conception rates run below the Lyrian baseline. A woman who reaches reproductive age has already shown a resilience the Wastelands don't allow the fragile, and one who carries a child to term and sees it live past infancy is valued more highly still (see Pregnancy and Death).

### Venom

The corruption that changed everything else about Ashwalkers changed their venom too. It no longer paralyzes as their ancestors' venom did, and it has none of the calming effect the Moonweavers developed or the corrupting bite of the Suneaters. What it does is subtler, and within their society, more useful.

Corruption has given the venom a Majik-reactive quality. Along with a wound, a bite leaves a trace of the biter's individual corruption signature in the tissue. Every Ashwalker's bioluminescent pattern is unique, and their venom carries the same signature. At the bite site it shows as a faint luminous mark that fades over days or weeks, depending on how saturated the biter is. Another Ashwalker can read that mark the way a tracker reads a footprint.

Outsiders rarely understand that the signature also works as a tracker. The corruption left in the tissue resonates back toward its source, a passive return signal the biter picks up through the same sense that warns them of threats. At low saturation this amounts to a vague pull in a general direction, useful at close range and little further. At high saturation the signal is stronger and sharper, and it can resolve across considerable distance into something close to an exact position. The visible mark fades, but the resonance lingers in the tissue far longer than anyone watching the mark disappear would guess.

A bite is therefore a weapon, a brand, and a declaration, and it also works as surveillance that leaves no visible trace once the mark is gone. By comparison the effect on the recipient is mild: a burning sensation, some local disorientation, and a brief fever. Causing pain was never the purpose.

### Ardor

The Ardor triggers faster in Ashwalkers and runs hotter. The environment keeps their bodies under constant strain, so the threshold for the second-wind response sits lower than in other Lyrians. Combined with the bioluminescent flare that comes with it, the result is impossible to hide or deny once it starts.

The crash afterward hits harder than in other Lyrian subspecies. Corruption-thickened tissue and constant environmental strain leave the post-Ardor window dangerously vulnerable, and every Ashwalker knows it and knows their enemies know it too. Learning to manage the onset, delaying and directing it and recovering faster, is one of the most useful skills an Ashwalker can develop. Some never manage it, and they tend to have brief and very dangerous careers.

### Diet

Ashwalkers eat what the Wastelands provide. That means mutated fauna, of which there is no shortage, along with fungi and adapted plants from the deeper ruins where something still grows in the dark. Their digestive systems have adapted to things most species would find toxic or inedible, including heavy metals, certain chemical compounds, and levels of corruption in food that would put a Moonweaver in the ground. Some knowingly eat corrupted food without concern, and their bodies handle it.

Preparation depends on what is available and who is cooking, and the cook isn't always skilled or patient. Meat is usually cooked directly over a fire of radiation-touched wood from dead Wasteland trees, which burns with a faint green tinge most Ashwalkers stopped noticing generations ago. The deeper ruins occasionally yield sealed pre-Cataclysm food stores that are still edible. These count as significant finds for a practical reason. A sealed container of old Lyrian rations is calories that cost nothing to get, and in the Wastelands that has value of its own.

## Behavior

### The Wasting Mind

The Wastelands inflict paranoia on anyone who enters them. For outsiders it is an assault, an environmental hazard as real as the radiation or the smog. Ashwalkers experience it differently. Generations of exposure have absorbed it into something closer to a threat assessment that never switches off, running beneath every conscious thought.

It shows in small ways that unsettle outsiders before they understand them. An Ashwalker stops mid-sentence to shift position relative to a door, or declines a seat with its back to open space and offers no explanation. They take in a room within moments of entering, registering and assessing every person without visible effort. Over generations the sense has sharpened past its origin until paranoia no longer describes it well. Ashwalkers speak of a knowing that arrives from no identifiable source, a certainty that the present moment holds a threat not yet visible, and they trust it the way other beings trust their eyes. The ones who learn to act on it before they can explain it are the ones who survive. Outsiders who spend enough time among them call it prophetic, and the Ashwalkers see no reason to correct them.

### The Debt Mind

Social life in the Wastelands runs on obligation: what is owed, what was given, what can be collected, and what failing to pay will cost. No courts or formal structures enforce any of it. A debt holds only as long as the creditor can make default painful, so the largest debt-holders are almost always the most capable people, and the system keeps reinforcing itself.

The nearest thing Ashwalker culture has to a sacred statement translates roughly as *I remember what I'm owed.* It works as patience, warning, and contract at once. Saying it acknowledges that an obligation exists and will be settled. Hearing it said to you carries weight, because everyone present understands what will happen if you ignore it.

Ashwalkers attach no moral shame to defaulting, but it is tactically catastrophic. The web of obligation that keeps any individual alive and supplied holds together only on the expectation of payment. Pull enough threads and it pulls away from you, and in the Wastelands that kills more reliably than any blade.

### Salvage Rights

What you pull from the ruins is yours. No law backs this, since nothing in the Wastelands has law behind it, yet Ashwalkers hold to it more firmly than most societies hold to their laws. An artifact you retrieve yourself belongs to you absolutely. No earlier claim overrides it and no authority can force you to hand it over. Anyone who tries to take it can expect a response that won't involve negotiation.

Ashwalkers don't manufacture technology in any meaningful sense. They repair and repurpose what they find. Two of them might carry weapons five hundred years apart in pre-Cataclysm development, because each carries what they personally retrieved. The resulting gap in individual capability is enormous, and it is a large part of why territory in the ruins is worth dying for.

### Strength and Its Performance

Among Ashwalkers, strength counts only while it is being shown. A reputation built yesterday is open to question today. Authority lasts exactly as long as it can be actively defended, and a position that goes unchallenged for long enough eventually draws someone eager to test whether it has gone soft.

Leaders hold their place by constantly demonstrating capability, a working network of obligations, and a greater capacity for danger than any rival. Once the demonstration lapses, erosion begins. A subordinate who refuses an order and survives has already opened the question of who is in charge. Wasteland rulers almost never get to retire.

### Compassion

Compassion exists among Ashwalkers. The Tai'shan is biological, and no amount of philosophy can talk it out of relevance. Their protectiveness toward children runs fierce enough that outsiders often mistake it for aggression. Two people who keep each other alive through what the long dark of the ruins throws at them form a bond the culture officially has no word for and unofficially understands completely.

They hide it because it can be used against them. Visibly caring about someone gives everyone watching a lever, so affection takes the same coded, underground form as the Tai'shan. The Ashwalkers who have lived longest are almost never the ones who care about nothing, though watching them would never reveal it.

## Majik

### The Corrupted Weave

Among Ashwalkers, Majik is less a discipline than a condition.

Every Lyrian carries a Matter Wave, the quantum-level frequency of their being that governs how they interact with the fabric of reality. Moonweavers cultivate that frequency through years of study until it is precise and controllable. Ashwalkers have spent two thousand years in a corruption field so dense that the line between the shaper and what is being shaped has blurred.

The corruption is part of them, in their tissue, their bioluminescence, and their venom. When an Ashwalker reaches toward the Weave, they draw on something that has been rewriting their Matter Wave since before they were born, and what comes back isn't always what they intended.

### Raw Shaping

Ashwalker shaping is powerful the way a structural collapse is powerful. Trained Moonweaver shapers find its results hard to explain and harder to counter, because it ignores the harmonic principles conventional Majik theory is built on. Corruption has shifted the Ashwalker baseline wavelength far enough that standard attunement models partly break down when applied to them. Their shaping often doesn't match what theory predicts, and when it misses, it usually overshoots.

The price is control. Fine manipulation of the kind Moonweaver enchanters spend years developing is largely beyond them. Their shaping is blunt, situational, and hard to aim. Those who learn to work within that limit become very dangerous. Those who fight it suffer Matter Wave Misalignment at rates that would end a Moonweaver's career and usually only inconvenience an Ashwalker, whose corrupted physiology absorbs ripple effects that would shatter a cleaner body.

Burnout from overextension also takes a different form in them. Instead of severing the attunement cleanly, it destabilizes it, leaving a stretch of time in which the passive corruption field in their body runs with no direction. Their bioluminescence flickers erratically and their threat-sense screams at nothing, and they perceive reality as fractured in ways that aren't entirely hallucination. The episode passes, but each one leaves something behind.

### Passive Resonance

Most Ashwalkers never formally shape at all. They carry something Moonweavers would classify as involuntary attunement and regard with professional unease.

Corruption has made their bodies into low-level Majik receptors, whether they want it or not. The bioluminescence that responds to emotion is the most visible sign, the body displaying its internal Majik field in real time, and the resonance goes deeper than that. Near Tear-adjacent spaces, corrupted zones, or dense crystal formations, an Ashwalker registers the place physically before they identify it consciously. It comes as pressure behind the eyes or a shift in the bioluminescent pattern, or as the threat-sense narrowing from its usual ambient awareness into something with a direction.

The same mechanism produces the knowing quality outsiders notice in them. It comes from reading the present at a resolution most beings can't reach, the corruption in their bodies resonating with distortions in reality the way a cracked bone aches under pressure before it gives.

### Crystals and the Corrupted

Standard Tear-crystals react unpredictably with Ashwalker physiology. The orange crystals that power conventional shaping and Veil-Engines carry a wavelength attuned to a stable reality, and an Ashwalker's Matter Wave no longer quite represents one. Extended contact with high-purity crystals is uncomfortable at low saturation and painful at high saturation.

What they can work with are the corrupted crystal formations found in the deepest Wasteland zones, black-green growths conventional enchanters won't touch. To an Ashwalker these resonate cleanly, since the corrupted wavelength matches their own altered frequency. Weapons and tools built around corrupted crystal are an exclusively Ashwalker craft, and the knowledge of how to work them is guarded as closely as a Delver's. In Ashwalker hands a corrupted-crystal weapon behaves in ways conventional Majik engineering says are impossible. The Moonweavers have spent considerable research effort trying to understand why, without getting close enough to the source material to find out.

## Relations

### Moonweavers

The Moonweavers regard the Ashwalkers with the particular discomfort of a people who share blood with something they would rather not acknowledge. The connection shows in bone structure, in the falling-horn lineage that still surfaces in some Ashwalker bodies, and in the fragments of old Elyric that survive in their corrupted dialect. The Moonweavers don't discuss it openly, and neither do the Ashwalkers.

Between them is a cold, practical relationship built on mutual need and presented as mutual contempt. The Moonweavers want what the Wastelands hold: pre-Cataclysm technology, particular artifacts, and access to ruins their own people won't enter. The Ashwalkers want things the Moonweavers can make, including medicine, textiles, refined crystals, and the occasional piece of intelligence about what is moving in the galaxy beyond Lyria. Trades happen. Neither side pretends to trust the other, which makes the deals cleaner than most.

Ashwalkers know about Moonweaver interference in unification efforts by instinct, if not by proof. That knowledge takes the form of a particular suspicion reserved for any Moonweaver operating near a consolidating clan, and the suspicion has proved right often enough to harden into policy.

Moving between the two peoples is possible but humiliating in both directions, and Ashwalker biology makes it worse. An Ashwalker can't just leave. Away from the Wastelands their saturation slowly drops toward the withdrawal described under Corruption Saturation, and what kept them functional in the ruins becomes a liability in cleaner air. Most who try to settle elsewhere come back within a year.

The Moonweavers know this, which is part of why they treat an Ashwalker trying to join their society as a negotiation problem instead of a security one. The biological leverage is all on their side.

It still happens. Now and then an Ashwalker with enough resources, cunning, or value to a clan that needs something from the Moonweavers manages to secure something more permanent than a trade agreement: a formal arrangement. These take months or years of hard negotiation, and they come about only when the Ashwalker has made themselves indispensable enough that the Moonweavers judge acceptance cheaper than refusal.

The Moonweaver who agrees to such an arrangement goes against the current of their own culture. Nothing forbids it, and nothing needs to, because the social cost is deterrent enough. The Ashwalker who arrives knows exactly what they are walking into and comes anyway, and that tells the Moonweavers something about them that isn't entirely reassuring.

The arrangement is never comfortable. The Ashwalker needs managed exposure to keep their saturation survivable, which means periodic trips back to the Wastelands that their Moonweaver partner can't always join. Their bioluminescence marks them in Moonweaver spaces, and no amount of time makes it less obvious. Their children, if they have any, carry the variety of both lineages and are watched with a fascination that isn't entirely kind. The Moonweavers accept them as working members of the community, because their doctrine demands it and because in the end they aren't cruel. They also never forget what came in with the arrangement, and they make sure the Ashwalker doesn't either.

### Suneaters

The relationship with the Suneaters is simpler and uglier. The Moonweavers at least keep a careful distance and call it diplomacy. The Suneaters don't bother. To them the Ashwalkers are corruption-touched degenerates squatting in ruins they had no part in building, too compromised to count as true Lyrians and too dangerous to dismiss. They make little effort to hide their contempt.

The Ashwalkers return it without ceremony. In the Wastelands, Suneater pride looks like posturing from people who can afford to care about honor because their environment hasn't yet stripped everything else away. Ashwalkers recognize the Ardor-fueled intensity Suneaters wear as identity, since they have it too, and have mostly learned to manage it as a liability. Watching the Suneaters celebrate it as a virtue leaves them with a quiet disdain.

Open conflict between the two is uncommon but not rare. The Dune Sea and the Wastelands share no real border, so friction is limited to the Outlands and whatever contact trade or desperation brings. When those encounters turn violent they tend to be decisive, because neither side fights the way the other expects.

Moving from the Wastelands to the Dune Sea is even less workable than moving toward the Moonweavers. The Suneaters don't negotiate. Nothing an Ashwalker can offer outweighs the physical revulsion their bioluminescence and corruption-scent provoke in Suneaters. An Ashwalker who approaches Suneater territory without significant leverage is treated as a threat or an intruder and dealt with accordingly.

The few who cross between the two peoples almost always do so through violence or desperation. A Suneater taken in the Outlands and brought into the Wastelands begins to change if they survive long enough, the corruption slowly working on a body never built for it. The bioluminescence that emerges in them is faint and irregular, nothing like the deep patterning of a born Ashwalker, but it is visible, and their own people won't take them back. An Ashwalker who ends up in Suneater hands fares worse. The Suneaters have no use for them that requires keeping them intact.
