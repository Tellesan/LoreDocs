# LYRIA
## Iriah Species Compendium

---

## Overview

The Iriah homeworld, Mirvaelos, was shattered by a Tear event at the First Cataclysm. Its two halves still hang in space, a scar visible from anywhere in the system. The same event that broke the planet flooded the survivors with a foreign Matter Wave and rewrote their biology. In the two thousand years since, the spacefaring that began as survival has become the center of who they are, culturally, spiritually, and physically. They could have settled somewhere by now. They keep wandering because they have become a people of the spaces between stars, and no single world fits them anymore.

Their population is small, but they are among the most recognizable species in known space, found along trade routes and in ports across the galaxy. Their navigational skill and accumulated knowledge are valuable enough that both major powers want their services, and neither fully trusts them. The Iriah work on contract, keep moving, and hold to a studied neutrality in the cold war between the Luminari Empire and the Galactic Protectorate. Neutrality pays them far better than alignment would.

## Society

### The Thessin

The Thessin, or Provider, is the person who acquired the ship and invites others to crew it. Their authority comes from responsibility. The ship belongs to them, and so does accountability for everyone aboard, which places them above the situational leadership of navigators and shapers.

An invitation to join a crew is personal as well as practical. Joining a ship means joining a household, and accepting carries the weight of a commitment the culture takes seriously. The Thessin feeds and shelters the crew, steers the ship toward opportunity, and makes the large decisions about where it goes and what it does. In return the crew gives skill, loyalty, and presence to something the Thessin is building. The relationship is closer to that of a household head than an employer, and it keeps that quality throughout.

In practice the role usually falls to males. Acquiring and holding a ship calls for a public display of authority, and the culture already reads that kind of physical weight in male bodies. Female Thessin are uncommon but not excluded, and they hold the role differently. A woman's gold mask makes her face legible to every Iriah she meets, and in formal settings it works as a credential, announcing who she is before she speaks. Male authority has no equivalent. A female Thessin's authority is more personal and harder to separate from her as an individual, which makes it more durable in some respects and more exposed in others.

### The Thessimura

The Thessimura is the ship family: everyone aboard, bound together by proximity and mutual dependence. Partnership and crew overlap here without the clean lines land-based cultures usually draw. Children are raised collectively, and every adult aboard shares responsibility for them. Romance and partnership happen inside this structure as a matter of course. Polycules are the norm, and exclusivity is unusual in a community that has always survived by having everyone pull their weight.

A new member joining an established Thessimura goes through a period somewhere between courtship and apprenticeship. The culture has little framework for the jealousy that comes with exclusive pairing, so a newcomer doesn't threaten existing bonds, but new connections are grown slowly instead of declared. The ship has to function, and a bond that destabilizes the existing ones is first of all a practical problem.

### The Flotilla

Above the ship sits the flotilla, which serves as a nation. Several operate across known space, each with its own history, reputation, and political character. Each ship keeps its identity and each Thessin keeps authority over their own vessel, but the flotilla is the collective they belong to and the flag they fly when dealing with outsiders.

Iriah hold flotilla identity with the pride and possessiveness other cultures reserve for nationality, and probably more fiercely. Few things in Iriah life are both mobile and permanent. A flotilla is one of them, moving through space while keeping an unbroken identity across centuries.

The Thessivaelor, the flotilla's council of Providers, governs above the ship level. Disputes that can't be resolved aboard a ship go there for judgment, and decisions affecting the whole flotilla are made there. The council uses its authority regularly, but it does not override a Thessin aboard their own vessel unless the flotilla's collective interest clearly outweighs the ship's autonomy. A dispute between a crew member and their Thessin that reaches the Thessivaelor reflects badly on both, however it ends.

### The Vaelorin

The Vaelorin, the Knowledge Keepers, are drawn mainly from those with weaker Weave connection. The path carries its own distinction and meets a need navigators and shapers can't. Someone whose attention isn't divided by strong attunement has a cleaner relationship with the written record and the oral tradition, and their sense of history isn't colored by the Weave.

A Vaelorin does more than keep archives. They carry the context the written record assumes its reader already has: why an entry was made the way it was, what the political climate was at the time, and what its author was known for, including their perspective and biases. To the Iriah a document is incomplete without that context, and the Vaelorin holds both.

Every flotilla keeps several. One rotates to Irivaeth for a fixed term, and the handover between rotating Vaelorin is among the most important ceremonies the culture observes. Over an extended period of shared residence at the station, the outgoing Keeper passes on everything that can't be written down. In flotilla deliberations a Vaelorin's authority comes from knowing what happened the last time a question came up, and the Iriah treat the past as their main evidence for handling the present.

In communal shaping, the Vaelorin carry the songs. Practitioners align their Weave attunement through coordinated song (see The Emirvouse), and the Vaelorin hold the melodic form exactly while the shapers fill it with power. Without a Vaelorin anchoring the coordination, individual voices drift and the collective effect weakens. The strongest shaper working without a skilled Vaelorin cannot match what a moderate one achieves with good coordination. The two roles depend on each other in the most important rituals, and that dependence is much of why the Vaelorin path is honored, even though it draws from people the culture would otherwise struggle to place.

### Ship Title History

Ships accumulate titles in the written record, formal designations that record significant deeds in compressed form. A title belongs to the ship, and it stays with the ship through crew changes and Thessin succession regardless of who earned it. It is the household's lineage, traced through deeds instead of blood.

Ships cannot grant themselves titles. The Vaelorin record them, and a title is only as credible as the entry behind it. Any Vaelorin who comes across an undocumented title notes it as unsubstantiated, and in a culture that treats the written record as the authority on what happened, that does more damage than having no title at all.

At major ceremonies a Vaelorin reads the ship's full title sequence in order, so the assembled Iriah know who they are dealing with before anyone has to explain themselves.

### Irivaeth, the Caliteth, and the Calumirva

Irivaeth is the station that floats between the two halves of Mirvaelos, the fixed spiritual center of an otherwise mobile culture. It is the oldest Iriah structure still standing. It has been maintained for two thousand years because keeping it alive beside what was lost is a sacred act, whatever its practical value.

Every Iriah must make the Caliteth to reach adulthood, navigating alone through the debris field between the two halves of the broken homeworld to reach the station. The field is active and treacherous. Two thousand years of gravitational interaction have churned the debris in ways no chart fully captures. At some point in the approach the instruments stop being enough, and the navigator has to feel the way through on spatial sense and instinct. Some do not make it, and the Iriah consider that part of what the ritual means.

At a traditional waypoint along the route, the navigator stops, puts on the Emirath, the ceremonial blindfold used in Majik practice, and holds position for a set period. It gives them time to feel the debris field through spatial sense alone, and to feel the Weave moving strangely near a place where reality was torn open two thousand years ago and never fully healed. What comes to them there is considered private. The culture asks them to be present for it and does not ask them to share it.

On arrival, the Calumirva requires the navigator to present something that furthers the Iriah as a people. Display and gesture don't count. The offering has to have cost effort, sacrifice, or discovery to produce, and it has to keep contributing long after the person who brought it is gone. The range of acceptable offerings is deliberately wide. Someone might bring a component that keeps the station running, a navigational discovery, a skill learned outside the flotilla, or a piece of oral tradition recovered or newly made. Every offering is recorded at the station alongside every other offering made since the first generation.

### Routes and Piracy

The Iriah choose their routes with care. Their favored lanes run through the outer systems, where Imperial enforcement is thinnest and slowest to arrive and local authority is weakest, and they picked those lanes for exactly that reason. Generations of flying them have taught Iriah crews where outside attention reaches and where it doesn't. The Luminari Stellar Enforcement Agency has noticed how closely Iriah routes track its own blind spots.

Some crews work in the gray space between legitimate commerce and outright crime, using claims to routes their ships have flown for generations to justify interdicting vessels that pass through. Officially, the culture looks the other way. A flotilla keeps its deniability as long as no Iriah ships are targeted and no one can trace the crew back to it.

Once a crew is identified and linked to its flotilla, it is expected to surrender, without exception. The flotilla's survival and standing outweigh any single crew's freedom. Surrender carries no dishonor among the Iriah. It settles a debt under the same framework that governs every other obligation, and this debt is the cost of having operated under the flotilla's implicit protection.

Iriah interdiction succeeds mostly on navigational advantage. Pirate crews know the routes and know where a ship will be before its crew realizes anyone is watching. An Emirvouse can also work the Weave around a target vessel in ways no standard countermeasure is built to handle, leaving navigation systems unreliable and Veil-Engine calculations wrong. The pirates always prefer that the target make a practical assessment before any demonstration is needed. Most targets do.

### The Internal Fracture

The committed wanderers, the larger faction, believe that two thousand years of spacefaring made the Iriah what they are and that settling would destroy it. Their fear goes beyond losing customs or traditions. They believe the wandering itself has shaped the Iriah character, and that giving it up would be an amputation.

The smaller faction looks at damaged worlds being restored elsewhere in the galaxy and argues that wandering only means something if it is chosen. A people with somewhere to return to, they say, would wander more freely than a people with nowhere. Two thousand years is long enough, and the culture is strong enough to put down roots without being swallowed by them.

People on both sides love the culture and believe they are arguing for its survival. The disagreement runs through ships and families, and the Iriah hold it the way they hold most unresolved questions, without forcing an answer. The Caliteth is the wanderers' strongest argument. Every adult alive has made that approach, sat blindfolded in the debris field of Mirvaelos, and felt what was lost. The other side can't dispute that experience. It can only argue that the experience might mean something other than what the wanderers insist.

## Bonds

Within the general warmth of the Thessimura, the culture recognizes particular bonds that stand apart from ordinary shipboard affection.

### The Solumath

The Solumath is a bond two people choose deliberately, in calm instead of crisis. It grows in the ordinary time between significant events, over shared meals and quiet shifts, as two people decide to know each other more completely than ship life requires. It exists because both chose it, and they keep it by continuing to choose it, without fuss, in the everyday moments aboard ship.

That makes it the most fragile of the bonds in one sense. It has no crisis to point to as its origin and no collective to shelter in, and it lasts only as long as both people keep choosing it. The culture regards that fragility with tenderness. Many Iriah consider a bond sustained by choice, day after day, braver than any bond formed under pressure.

### The Orumath

The Orumath forms without anyone choosing it, in conditions that leave no room for calculation. Two people keep each other alive through something that would have broken either of them alone and come out bound, whether or not either would have sought the connection otherwise. It may be romantic, but often it isn't. What defines it is the weight of having been completely known by someone at a moment when there was nothing left to perform.

An Orumath cannot be ended the way a Solumath can. No biological mechanism holds it in place. The experience that formed it can't be undone, so the two people carry the bond for life, whatever becomes of their relationship. Distance, conflict, and time change how it shows, but it remains.

## Appearance

The Iriah are striking and plainly non-human, recognizable across a room without a second look. Their skin runs deep blue, violet, purple, or forest green, with a natural iridescence that shifts with the angle of the light, so the color never looks quite the same twice. Over that lies the gold flecking, a second, metallic layer of luminescence that tells those who can read it far more than it shows at a glance.

The sexes look very different. Males are tall and lean, with the species' dextrous build on a larger frame and functional muscle that stays close to the bone without bulk. Females are much smaller and finer, fragile-looking by comparison, built for precision more than strength. Both are clearly the same species, yet they don't read as one body type at two sizes.

In males the gold flecking gathers along the jaw, cheekbones, and temples, the inner wrists, and the backs of the hands, wherever the biology sits close to the surface. In females the pattern is different in kind. The flecks across the face merge into something dense and continuous that covers the area around the eyes, spreads across the cheekbones, and runs down toward the jaw, forming what reads as a mask. Nothing like it appears elsewhere on the body. Every woman's mask is unique, and to those trained in it, far more readable than flecking anywhere else. When a Vaelorin looks at a female Iriah's face, they see her Weave signature in its densest and most legible form, with her lineage and the contributors to her gestation written in the gold.

As a result, a female Iriah cannot be anonymous among her own people the way a male can. Any Iriah who sees her face knows her identity, her lineage, and her Weave expression without having to ask. Vaelindrath entries for women tend to carry richer biometric detail than men's for the same reason. The record reflects what her face already tells anyone who knows how to look.

The bone crest is a cartilaginous ridge running from the forehead back over the skull, low enough to look elegant and high enough to be visible at any range. In males the gold flecking is densest along the crest, which catches and holds light differently from the surrounding skin. In females the crest is finer, and its gold flows into the upper edge of the face mask. What an Iriah does with their crest is a personal matter. Some leave it alone, some fit dark metalwork flat against it to catch and reflect the gold, and some reshape the structure itself over years. These modifications usually carry private meaning and are rarely just ornament.

The eyes are large, with gold or amber irises that fill most of the visible eye and leave very little white. In low light the iris gathers whatever light is available and seems faintly luminous, which most beings find striking and a few find unsettling. A thin secondary membrane slides across the eye horizontally, filtering light in environments unlike the artificial shipboard lighting the body is adapted to. It closes partway in strong natural light, retracts fully in near darkness, and closes involuntarily during active shaping.

Hair comes in the same palette as the skin, from deep blue-black through purples to dark greens, with silver or pale violet at the lighter end. It carries no cultural meaning and is kept for practicality. The fingers are longer than the human baseline, and the gold gathers along the joints and knuckles. The ears sit high on the skull, with swept, refined cartilage that turns slightly toward any sound of interest, a beat before the eyes follow.

## Biology

### Bodies and Gravity

Two thousand years of shipboard life have adapted the Iriah body to the place it lives. Over a lifetime, bones and the cardiovascular system calibrate to the gravity setting of the ship, and that setting becomes the body's baseline. Through youth and middle age an Iriah can still handle planetary surface gravity, uncomfortably but without serious trouble. As the decades pass the body commits further to its ship, and the range of gravity it can tolerate narrows each year. For an elder who hasn't set foot on a planet in thirty years, the attempt would be dangerous.

Each flotilla's primary ship runs at lower gravity to keep its elders active and present through their final decades. That makes the primary ship the social and political center of the flotilla. Ceremonies are held there, and the Thessivaelor meets there. Younger crew visiting it notice the lighter gravity right away, and the ship feels different from a working vessel in more than the physical sense.

### The Gold

The gold flecking is the visible mark of the Majik exposure that altered the Iriah Matter Wave two thousand years ago, the Weave expressed through the skin.

When an Iriah is injured, the Majik concentrates at the wound while it heals and leaves a permanent trace in the repaired tissue. Gold runs along the scar lines and makes the damage visible and luminous. Older Iriah carry these marks in patterns that map a lifetime of hard moments, and the culture does not consider them disfiguring. A record of what someone has survived is honest in the same way the written record is honest.

During pregnancy the gold intensifies sharply. As the child's own Weave attunement begins to form, the body's Majik output rises, and the change shows. Iridescence that normally needs direct light becomes visible in ordinary conditions, the skin shifts more actively, and the gold brightens until it glows. In the last weeks before birth, a pregnant Iriah gives off enough light to navigate a dark room by. After the birth the gold settles back to its usual level.

At death the gold fades gradually as the Majik releases its hold on the individual at its own pace. By the time the body goes to the growing bays, it is gone.

### Echolocation and Spatial Awareness

The modified ear processes the surrounding sound continuously, building a passive spatial picture from vibration, pressure differences, and resonance without any conscious effort. In the immediate space this picture is complete and detailed. An Iriah knows where everyone is without looking, tracks movement without paying attention to it, and can get around in total darkness. Through an open doorway into an adjoining space the picture is coarser. Presence and broad movement still register, but identifying individuals takes enough noise to work with. Beyond that range the sense becomes impressionistic, a feeling that a space is occupied without any precise location.

The sense is richest in enclosed, familiar places. A ship someone has lived aboard for years gives the densest picture, since every resonance is known and anything unusual stands out against that baseline. Open planetary environments shrink the range considerably, because sound scatters instead of reflecting.

During Seval the spatial sense expands in both range and resolution. A Thessin in that state who knows the crew well can locate particular people anywhere on the ship by heart rhythms learned over years of living together, the Weave attunement amplifying what the ears report beyond anything instruments can match.

### Seval

Under enough stress, most species turn outward and grow aggressive, agitated, and physical. The Iriah go the other way. The Weave attunement that normally runs beneath conscious thought floods forward, spatial awareness sharpens to full resolution, and the mind goes very quiet and very clear. To an outsider the person seems to become unnervingly calm at exactly the wrong moment. The gold brightens slightly and the eyes settle into a fixed focus that means the spatial sense is at full capacity. When they speak, the voice drops into its lower register, unhurried and precise.

The body treats a threat the way it treats the meditative state the Emirath produces. Under enough pressure the same perceptual channel opens automatically, with no deliberate practice required.

The cost comes afterward. Running the Weave attunement at full capacity drains something that only quiet and time restore, and the spatial sense winds down slowly once the state releases. An Iriah who has held Seval for a long stretch needs stillness afterward, and the culture understands and allows for this without asking for explanation.

### Reproduction

Conception starts the pregnancy, which lasts twelve to thirteen months. About every three months the body enters a heat state, a biological signal that the next stage of development needs another contribution to continue. There are four contributions in all, each matching a stage in the child's formation.

The contributions can come from the same partner every time or from different people. A closely bonded pair carrying the whole gestation between them is entirely viable and regarded warmly. Drawing on several contributors is just as valid, and more common within the ship family structure the culture is built around. The Thessimura plans around the pregnancy's intervals as a practical matter and meets each heat period the way it meets any other need aboard ship.

The gold pattern that forms on the child during gestation carries traces of each contributor, setting progressively over the four stages. The finished pattern is a literal record of the child's origin, readable to anyone trained to interpret it. No two patterns match, because no two gestations have the same contributors in the same order.

### Lifespan and Diet

The Majik running through Iriah biology has lengthened their lives, mostly by stretching the active middle years. Iriah remain fully capable and keep wandering well into what other species would call old age. When the final decades do come, they spend them aboard the flotilla's primary ship, contributing knowledge and counsel in place of navigation and physical work.

Shipboard life made the Iriah vegetarian out of necessity, and they have been vegetarian for two thousand years. Every ship runs hydroponic growing bays as core infrastructure, and over the generations cultivation has become as much art as agriculture. Some varieties exist nowhere else, bred for artificial light and recycled air. Recipes go into the written record alongside navigational data and title histories, and the loss of a ship's culinary tradition is mourned as a cultural loss.

### Death

The body is returned to the growing bays, though not right away. The Thessimura keeps the deceased in the communal space for a period it decides together, usually between one and three days, depending on the person and the circumstances. During that time a Vaelorin makes the final entry in the record, the most important ceremony of the death process. The Vaelorin speaks the full name in formal register and reads the complete title history of every ship the person served on, in order. Then they list the person's contributions to the written record and name each bond they held, with the bond-mates present.

Once the record is closed, the body goes to the growing bays. The bay is chosen with care, and the plants are picked for meaning instead of yield. The Thessimura tends those plants knowing what they grow from, and the living and the dead share the ship in the most literal sense.

Someone who dies away from the ship receives the full ceremony anyway. The record is closed just as completely and a growing bay is still prepared. A missing body does not leave the ceremony incomplete, because to the Iriah the record is the person, and a record can always be finished.

## Behavior

### Presence in a Room

The constant passive mapping gives the Iriah a physical presence outsiders often find hard to read until they understand where it comes from. An Iriah walks into a room already knowing where everyone is. They settle near doorways without seeming to think about it, and they don't bump into things or people. In conversation they keep track of the room while holding eye contact, aware of what is happening behind them the whole time.

None of this is put on. It is how they experience space, and lacking the uncertainty most beings carry about their surroundings gives them an ease that reads as confidence.

### The Written Record Reflex

The Iriah write everything down. The habit runs so deep it works below the level of conscious choice. An Iriah who sees something significant records it, wants every negotiation documented, and starts a log the moment they arrive somewhere new. In the individual, the tradition that preserves the species' collective history shows up as someone who trusts documentation above every other form of truth.

The Iriah don't hold outsiders who lack this habit in contempt, but they are mildly wary of them. People who don't write things down tend to remember them differently from how they happened, and the Iriah have two thousand years of evidence on that point.

### Communal Instinct and Outsiders

Growing up in a Thessimura makes the Iriah collective-minded in their daily lives. This comes from pragmatism more than idealism. The ship runs when everyone contributes, so contribution is assumed and never negotiated. Someone who doesn't pull their weight is a practical problem first, and a moral one only later.

The same applies to guests. Iriah hospitality is sincere, and guests are brought into daily ship life, but they are expected to contribute however their abilities allow. A guest who finds ways to be useful is treated with growing warmth. One who doesn't is kept at a growing distance.

The Iriah don't perform sociability. Their melodic spoken language makes them pleasant to listen to whatever they're saying, which can mislead outsiders about how warm they are. In practice they are direct and pragmatic, and curious about anyone who knows something they don't. Navigational data and route knowledge interest them most, but skill in any field earns their respectful attention, and that attention is about as high a compliment as they pay.

They are also hard to deceive at close range. Their spatial sense reads the body as well as the words, and a raised heart rate or a change in breathing registers as clearly as movement does. That doesn't make them immune to lies, but it makes some kinds hard to sustain face to face, and in trade the Iriah have a well-earned reputation for knowing when a negotiation isn't entirely honest.

## Majik

### The Weave Attunement

The Tear event that destroyed Mirvaelos flooded the surviving population with a foreign Matter Wave, and it has run through Iriah biology ever since. Other forms of Majik exposure change the body gradually. This one arrived all at once, with nothing in place to receive it. The Iriah woke up changed and have spent two thousand years learning what they became, through experience more than theory.

Their tradition is spatial and navigational. They feel the Weave the way they feel a room, as a constant stream of spatial information. They can tell where it is thin or dense, where it is damaged, and where something is moving through it. That makes them exceptional at finding paths no instrument can chart and at sensing Tear activity before any device registers it. They can move through Weave-unstable environments that would disorient other practitioners, and they can shift the Weave around something so that Majik has trouble locating it.

Force is largely beyond them. Applying Majik directly as raw power sits mostly outside their capability, and against a practitioner whose tradition runs toward force they are outmatched in that kind of contest. This is part of why Iriah conflict strategy almost never involves direct confrontation.

### The Emirath

The Emirath is the ritual blindfold. Removing sight takes away the dominant sense competing with the Weave attunement, so the spatial awareness runs at full capacity without visual processing sharing the same channels. For a species whose spatial sense works as well as sight in familiar places, wearing the blindfold feels like focus. The physical world recedes, and the Weave becomes the main channel through which they perceive reality.

Advanced practitioners learn to reach this state without the blindfold, keeping sight available but in the background while the attunement runs at full strength. The Iriah consider this mastery, and it is rare.

An Iriah's Emirath is among their most treasured possessions. Its material, its history, who made it, and what it has been present for all add to its meaning over a lifetime of use, and receiving one as a gift carries great weight. The Emirath appears at the waypoint on the Caliteth, in every formal communal working, and in daily individual practice.

### The Emirvouse

An Emirvouse, or Chorus, is a group of practitioners working together through coordinated song. The spoken language is melodic, and its tonal shifts carry grammatical meaning, so it already sits close to song. In an Emirvouse those tones become the mechanism that keeps individual attunements in step and turns them into one coherent expression.

Without coordination, several practitioners feeling the same Weave space at once would interfere with each other. Song solves this. The shared melody brings each person's expression into line the way instruments tune to a common pitch, and the combined signal can do things no single practitioner can. How precisely the group stays in step determines how strong the result is.

Every participant in a formal working wears the Emirath. With everyone blindfolded, they share a single Weave space, and the song gathers what each spatial sense perceives into one coherent picture. Those who have taken part describe the experience as unlike anything else they know.

A Vaelorin carries the song itself, holding its melodic form while the practitioners supply the power, as described under The Vaelorin.

## Relations

### Luminari Empire

The Iriah relationship with the Luminari Empire is commercial first, with everything else a distant second. The Empire needs navigators who can go where its own people can't, who can approach infrastructure that takes specialist knowledge to reach safely, and who can guide ships through Tear-adjacent space that ordinary crews find dangerous. The Iriah provide all of this on contract and on their own terms. They take no permanent posts and align politically no further than the transaction at hand.

The Moonweavers hold a separate and much closer place. Two thousand years of working together have built something well beyond commerce, a shared intellectual tradition around navigation and the preservation of knowledge, with the Iriah written record and the Moonweaver oral tradition each filling gaps in the other. Both cultures have come to rely on that. The Iriah closest to Moonweaver culture land on Lyria regularly, in the canyon city, keeping up relationships centuries older than the current political order.

The Empire's preference for permanent, hierarchical staffing clashes with Iriah contracting culture at every turn. The Empire has learned to build its arrangements around Iriah terms instead of trying to change how the Iriah work, though it has not always done so gracefully.

### Galactic Protectorate

Relations with the Protectorate are harder. The Protectorate abhors Majik and those who practice it, and the Weave attunement in Iriah biology is exactly what its ideology treats as a threat to be managed. It needs Iriah navigators badly enough to work around this in practice while keeping its ideological position in public. The result is mutual pragmatism and mutual distrust in roughly equal measure.

The Protectorate keeps detailed files on Iriah ship title histories, gathered through intelligence operations. The Iriah know and find it more amusing than worrying, since those histories were never secret and are read aloud at every major ceremony.
