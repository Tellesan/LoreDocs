# LYRIA
### The Suneaters

---

## The Dune Sea

### At a Glance

The Suneaters are the Lyrians of the Dune Sea. Their clans answer to a single Matriarch, whose own clan, the Kenivar-Jho, sits at the center of Suneater power.

Suneaters have occupied, fought over, fed on, and ritually wounded the Dune Sea for longer than most surviving histories can prove. So much blood has soaked into the land that the Weave no longer lies cleanly across it. It catches and thins, tears, mends badly, and tears again. Blood spilled once in a place may stain it. Blood spilled there for thousands of years turns the place into a wound with its own geography.

Trained shapers find the Weave beneath the Dune Sea hard to predict. It may resist a careful hand one moment and collapse under a careless thought the next. Crystals carried into the region hum off-pattern, crack in their settings, or bleed heat through their metal casings. Sensitive horns ache for hours before a Tear forms, and sometimes for hours after it closes. Those with natural Weave-sense describe it as standing on cloth stretched too thin over something that moves.

The Dune Sea remembers what has been done to it, and the memory acts on whoever walks there.

### Day and Night

From orbit the Dune Sea is a vast pale-gold desert broken by black stone ridges, glassed basins, old ritual roads, and the skeletons of structures too large for any living clan to have built. On the ground it feels wrong. The air seems to breathe out of rhythm, and shadows gather beneath objects too small to cast them. Nothing about the desert is natural.

Day cycles here are punishingly long, and the light arrives in layers instead of hours. When the first sun climbs, the land begins to bake. When the second follows, the heat stops behaving like weather and becomes a pressure on the skin, the lungs, the eyes, and the horns. Sand turns bright enough to blind, and stone holds heat until it rises in a visible shimmer. Metal can't be touched barehanded. Water is guarded as carefully as blood.

The Suneaters were shaped under this and take it as proof of their right to the land. They endure the day better than most, drawing strength from the suns in ways even they can't always separate from faith. Their bodies run hot, their sunfire catches more easily, and their confidence sharpens under open light. A Suneater at noon in the Dune Sea can still be killed, but the land does its best to make everyone forget it.

Night cycles are rarer and shorter, and they bring relief and danger together. Once both suns withdraw, the temperature drops fast, bleeding heat out of the sand and stone until the desert is cold enough to kill the unprepared. Night leaves the territory as dangerous as ever and only shifts who holds the advantage.

Moonweaver Hunters come in during night cycles, when they come at all. Darkness cools the land, dulls the immediate strength of sunfire, and gives Moonweaver discipline room to work. They move under lunar cover with protective silk, Hunter-Seer guidance, and military precision. They strike outposts, recover captives, collapse Ritual sites, and burn ghouls, and they take small pieces of territory before the suns return and make staying too costly.

The Suneaters hate these incursions bitterly. The Moonweavers don't always win, since the Dune Sea kills disciplined soldiers as readily as reckless ones when the Weave turns wrong beneath them. Every successful night raid still proves what Suneaters would rather not admit, that their dominion has hours when it weakens.

### The Night Day

Once every few hundred years, Lyria's cycles align into the Night Day. Both suns line up and the moon eclipses them, plunging the Dune Sea into a darkness deeper and longer than any ordinary night. Generations can live and die on stories of it without ever seeing one. Predicting it takes records, mathematics, and the Weave-sense to read the sky correctly, and few Suneaters have all three. Even those who can predict it never fully trust how the Dune Sea will behave once it arrives. The land remembers too much violence to turn orderly because the heavens have.

To Suneaters, the Night Day is humiliation written across the sky. Their bodies diminish under it without failing. Sunfire becomes harder to summon and costlier to hold, and without the steadying glare of the suns, horn-sensitivity sharpens until it hurts. Ritual sites behave unpredictably, and ghouls stir more aggressively. Errant Tears open and close in the dark more often, like wounds blinking awake. Without its burning masters, the Dune Sea stops behaving the way Suneaters expect.

The Matriarchal court treats the Night Day as a trial, an omen, and a threat. Publicly it is framed as a test of blood, a chance to prove that true Suneater strength doesn't depend on sunlight alone. Privately, clans secure their stores, arm their claimed males, and watch their daughters closely. Lesser clans are pressed into renewed oaths. Temples are sealed or readied, depending on what each priest thinks the darkness will wake.

Moonweavers see it as an opportunity. During the Night Day they move in greater numbers than they would dare in an ordinary cycle. Hunter-Seers guide military forces along routes too dangerous in daylight, and wardens, cleansers, soldiers, and scouts advance behind them to secure footholds, destroy active Ritual grounds, recover the taken, and push deeper into Suneater territory than ordinary night raids allow.

They aren't after conquest for its own sake. The Moonweavers believe Lyria can't heal while the Dune Sea remains an open wound and the Suneaters remain bound to the Matriarch's corruption. That makes the Night Day one of the oldest points of contention between the two peoples. Suneaters prepare for it with pride, dread, and fury, and Moonweavers prepare with patience measured in centuries. Whole strategies are built around a darkness most soldiers will only hear about from elders. Clans rise or fall by how well they endure it. In the dark, captives disappear and temples burn, and daughters make choices they could never make under the suns.

### The Breaking of Weaker Minds

Madness is common enough in the Dune Sea that Suneaters don't romanticize it. In their view a weak or careless mind breaks open, and a mind that listens too long starts answering things that were never speaking to it. Suneaters hold that the voice is Lyria's own, and that only the worthy can listen without breaking.

Corruption here often starts with perception before it touches the body. Travelers catch movement at the edge of vision or hear their names spoken from under the sand. They smell blood where none was spilled, lose time crossing dunes that should take minutes, dream while awake, and remember things that never happened to them.

For the untrained, fear is the smaller danger. In places the Weave is thin enough that a thought can catch on the wrong pattern and make something real. Grief can open a doorway, hunger can turn into instruction, and guilt can find a voice. A mind desperate to make sense of what it is touching builds explanations from fragments of other realities, ancestral echoes, ritual residue, and its own buried instincts. Some travelers recover if they are pulled out quickly. Some come back quieter, and some come back changed in ways no healer can name. Others never come back as themselves.

Suneaters resist this better than most, though not completely. Their blood has lived under the Dune Sea's pressure long enough to bear it, and their culture teaches children early not to flinch at hungers that would horrify outsiders. Hearing Lyria's voice is an honor, and staying sane while she speaks earns open admiration. That resistance lets them cross country that would break a foreign caravan in a day.

Moonweaver Hunter-Seers are trained specifically against it. They cross the Dune Sea with disciplined thought, controlled breathing, and emotional restraint honed into armor. They answer no voices and follow no familiar figures glimpsed at a distance, and they never touch exposed crystal growths barehanded, however clean the resonance seems. The first lesson of crossing Suneater territory is that not every thought in your head belongs to you.

### Thin Places

Thin places are common across the Dune Sea, though not always visible. Some are old ritual grounds where blood has been spilled so often that the Weave never thickened again. Some are battlefields, and some are ancient roads of blackened stone where processions once led victims with covered faces to the sound of bells. Others are nameless hollows in the sand where nothing grows or nests and no shadow falls correctly at noon.

A thin place may announce itself with heat, pressure, horn-ache, a metallic taste, or the sudden stillness of every living thing nearby. It may also give no sign at all until a traveler puts a foot where reality has grown too fragile to bear ordinary weight, and what follows is Suneater-corrupted Majik at its worst.

Suneaters value thin places because they make strong Majik easier. Ritual demands less sacrifice there, sunfire catches faster, blood rites run hotter, and abandoned dead rise more easily. A skilled hand can use a thin place to widen a working that would fail anywhere else. A careless one can open something they can't close.

Lesser clans fight over the known thin places, and the Kenivar-Jho claim the oldest and deepest outright. Holding such ground means holding access to power, and in the Dune Sea power is never left unguarded unless something worse than a guard already lives there.

### The Errant Tears

Tears in the Dune Sea don't always behave as they do elsewhere. In more stable regions a Tear is an event, a threshold, or a catastrophe. Here some of them come and go like weather. A seam of light opens between dunes for three breaths and shows a glimpse of another reality. A black slit appears above a ruined altar and spills acid rain that falls upward before it vanishes. A doorway forms beneath the sand and breathes out insects made of glass, a voice speaking an alien language, or a corpse that hasn't died yet. Then it closes.

Often the only warning is a change in pressure and a sudden wrongness in the world around it. The Weave opens because it is weak, because it was weakened before, because something on the other side pushed at the right moment, or because the Dune Sea has forgotten it is meant to be one reality at a time.

Much of what comes through doesn't survive. Many things collapse, burn, suffocate, or come apart once Lyria's laws reassert themselves. Others adapt fast, and some were never alive by any Lyrian definition. The Dune Sea holds things that don't belong to Lyria, don't understand it, and have no interest in being understood by what they hunt.

That is one reason the Suneaters control the Dune Sea less completely than they claim. They rule its people and command its roads, temples, and clan holdings. They hold no command over whatever crossed through a Tear and learned the taste of this reality before the wound closed behind it.

### The Underground Temples

The oldest Suneater temples are underground because no one trusted the surface to keep secrets. Some are reached by carved stairways buried beneath dunes, and some through fissures in black glass basins left by ancient sunfire. Others are entered through the mouths of colossal half-buried statues whose faces the sand has worn smooth. A few can be reached only when the Weave thins in a particular way, and the entrance appears where the stone remembers being open.

The architecture is heavy, hot, and close. Corridors are cut through dark volcanic stone, sun-baked clay, glassed sand, and mineral-veined rock that hums faintly where old Rituals soaked into the walls. Some passages have low ceilings, and others open into chambers high enough to swallow sound. Light comes from braziers, sunfire basins, crystal veins, and molten channels kept alive by old workings. The colors are seldom gentle: red-gold and furnace orange, bruised violet where the Weave has thinned too far, and now and then the white glare of a sealed chamber that still remembers a sacrifice powerful enough to burn without fuel. The air moves unevenly. Some rooms are breathlessly still, and others carry wind from no surface opening.

The altars are practical. There are slabs with channels cut for blood, pits where bodies were rendered, and rings of horn, bone, crystal, and metal set around places where the Weave wounds most easily. The oldest chambers are carved with genealogies, victories, devouring rites, and the names of daughters who rose too soon or not soon enough. In the royal temples, the Matriarch's history has been carved over older histories so many times that the walls are hard to read.

Not every temple is active. Rival clans sealed some, failed Rituals emptied others, and some are avoided because the dead inside have taken them over. Suneaters won't admit to fearing these places. They call them unready, contaminated, politically inconvenient, or claimed by the Ancestors, and anyone who hears movement behind the sealed doors finds the distinction makes little difference.

### Temple Cults and Keepers

No temple lasts long without keepers. Some are Hands of the Matriarch, trained in bloodline records, sacrifice, omen-reading, and the controlled weakening of the Weave. Others are lesser clan ritualists allowed to maintain local sites so long as their work doesn't threaten Kenivar-Jho supremacy. The oldest kind are ghoul-burners who know which sealed halls must never be opened, and attendants who have spent so long underground that daylight panics them instead of comforting them.

Temple keepers aren't always powerful shapers. Many hold their power because they know what not to touch. Over time the temple becomes an extension of its keeper, who knows how many steps each passage has, which doors open only in the presence of Majik, and which altar accepts lesser blood and which will punish the insult. The Matriarch tolerates the keepers because replacing them would be costly, and she distrusts them for the same reason.

### Ghouls of the Dune Sea

A risen Suneater corpse is dangerous anywhere (see Death), but the Dune Sea gives it everything it needs to become worse. The Weave is thin, the ground is soaked in old blood and ritual residue, and living prey is never far. The Ancestors pressing at the dead here might be ancestral spirits, corrupted memory, or something from beyond Lyria wearing a familiar shape because the dead mind supplied one.

A ghoul that has fed enough to speak with an Ancestor's voice is a political problem as well as a monster. It may offer knowledge long lost, or deceive the unwary and take a living body in place of a dead one. Most ghouls in the Dune Sea are burned quickly for that reason.

### The Non-Native Things

Some of what lives in the Dune Sea came through Tears. Some of those things died on arrival and left alien bones of no familiar shape. Some survived long enough to breed once, badly, within whatever local law of reality allowed it. Others never bred but persist, hidden in ruins, temple vents, glass fields, salt caves, and the deep cool spaces under old dunes.

Suneaters sort these things by usefulness first and origin second. They want to know whether a thing can be eaten, hunted, or used in Ritual, whether its blood weakens the Weave, and whether its hide resists sunfire or acid rain. The rarest questions are whether it speaks, whether it bargains, and whether it remembers a Tear from the far side.

There are dune-stalkers that cast no shadow except at night, and pale burrowing things that imitate the heartbeat of wounded prey beneath the sand. Flocks of knife-winged carrion creatures cry with a grief strong enough to lure the starving away from camp. Rooted organisms pass for dead trees until touched, then unfold into mouths built for realities with different rules for flesh. After errant Tears, glass insects appear and arrange themselves into symbols no Suneater can read.

Some of these species have become part of the Dune Sea's ecology. Others are lone accidents that haven't died yet. A few are intelligent enough to understand territory, tribute, and fear. Suneaters refuse to call them neighbors, which would imply equality. They call them hazards, resources, omens, or sport, depending on how many Suneaters have died learning otherwise.

### Travel

Nobody travels the Dune Sea casually. Routes are inherited, bought, stolen, or taken from the dead. Safe passages shift after major Rituals, clan wars, Tear events, or ghoul migrations, and a road one generation marked reliable may lead the next into a basin where the Weave has thinned enough to spill something else into it. A map is useful only alongside someone who knows which parts of it are lies told for safety.

Caravans travel with horn-readers, ghoul-burners, water guards, and at least one person whose only job is to watch the others' minds for signs of breaking. Sudden cheerfulness draws suspicion, and so does a silence that lasts too long. A traveler who insists a relative is standing beyond the firelight draws the most, especially if that relative is known to be dead.

Suneaters cross it better than outsiders because they understand its hungers and share some of them. Moonweaver Hunters cross it better than most Suneaters like to admit, because discipline works as armor. Everyone else survives through preparation, luck, payment, or the mercy of things not hungry enough at the moment to investigate.

### The Suneater Claim

The Suneaters claim the Dune Sea on the grounds that no one else has held it so long. The Dune Sea shaped them as much as they shaped it. Its scarcity hardened their hunger, and its thin places taught them Ritual. Its errant Tears fed their myths, their bloodlines, their enemies, and their nightmares. Its dead refused to stay dead, and the Suneaters built doctrine around the problem until the doctrine looked like destiny.

Ruling the Dune Sea has never meant mastering it. The Matriarch knows this, though she would kill most people who said it aloud. Her temples sit over wounds she didn't make and can't fully close, and her priests perform Ritual in places that answer too eagerly. Her warriors hunt ghouls wearing the faces of her own blood. Her daughters will inherit a throne along with a land taught for millennia that violence is the language of power.

The Dune Sea listens when Suneaters speak. That does not mean it obeys.

## Biology

### Horns

Few Lyrians have horns as soft, strange, or vulnerable as a female Suneater's. In the oldest bloodlines the horns serve as ornaments, weapons, and signs of maturity, and they are also sensory organs attuned to the Weave, living structures that detect pressure, instability, and weakness in the reality around them.

The outer casing is soft and fleshy, and it swells with blood during exertion, Ardor, fear, rage, or active shaping. Beneath it lies the core, rigid enough to hold the horn's shape but hollow, and far more fragile than the horn structures of most other Lyrian branches. A female's horns give her heightened sensitivity to the Weave at a cost. In battle they are an obvious target, and a clean strike can disorient her, blind her, or cut her connection to the surrounding Weave long enough for a disciplined opponent to survive what should have killed them. Suneater horns never regrow, so a horn damaged or lost in either sex stays that way for life.

Suneaters don't speak kindly of this weakness. A woman who can't protect her horns can't protect her claim. Royal daughters learn early to guard their horns without seeming to, and to punish anyone foolish enough to reach for them. Their saying holds that what is sensitive must never appear soft.

Male horns are usually thicker, sturdier, and more rigid. Most males lack the innate Weave connection of the female lines, and their horns reflect it. They sense the physical world more than the Weave: pressure, vibration, heat, movement, and proximity. The outer layer is still softer than true bone, but it doesn't swell or react with the intensity seen in Weave-touched females.

A male born with a true connection to the Weave is marked by it. His horns are often softer, more responsive, and more vulnerable, closer to the female pattern than the usual male one. Depending on who finds him first, such a male is valuable, dangerous, or politically inconvenient. Lesser clans may hide him, protect him, trade him, or send him upward as proof of loyalty. In Kenivar-Jho territory, nothing born useful stays private for long.

### Venom

Suneater venom corrupts by nature. In Lyrians it drives the dual hearts with violent intensity, pushes the body toward Ardor, and fixes heightened emotion on whoever delivered the bite. Whatever rises in the bitten body rises toward the one who marked it. Suneaters bite selectively for this reason. Once bitten, the drive to prove worth, secure a place, and answer the claim can overwhelm restraint in anyone undisciplined or unprepared.

Females usually make the claim. A powerful female may hold several claimed males, especially in royal lines, where her secure power draws males to judge her worthy of their protection. She seldom lets a bite break her own skin in return. Mutual biting is possible, but it implies vulnerability and equality, and Suneater society discourages its ruling women from showing either openly.

Traditional pair-bonding lives at the margins of Suneater society. Suneaters understand possession more readily than partnership and lineage more readily than romance. Love does happen, but where it appears, it has to be hidden.

### Outsider Envenomation

To other species, Suneater venom is almost always lethal. Their bodies aren't built to take that kind of corruptive Matter Wave imprint and stay intact. Hearts fail, blood overheats, and the nervous system collapses under signals it can't interpret.

Survivors are marked for life. The venom doesn't make them Suneater, but it teaches body and mind the shape of Suneater hunger. Dark instincts get easier to justify, desire gets harder to deny, and ambition sharpens. The survivor has felt what it is like to have every locked-away want set free, and they often become fixated on the one who bit them, needing to stay near them at any cost.

### Ardor

Suneater venom is one of the most reliable triggers of Ardor among Lyrians, and the state it produces fixes on the biter as described under Venom. In controlled circumstances this can reinforce claim, mating, loyalty, and dominance. Out of control, it can be catastrophic.

Suneaters see this as proof of strength. Among royal women, inducing Ardor through venom is a formal assertion of preference and possession. A male chosen this way is being tested, marked, and drawn into the female's political orbit as well as desired. Whether the claim lasts or he is discarded depends on how well he survives her attention, meets her expectations, and stays useful after the fever passes.

The aftermath is rarely discussed honestly. Ardor burns through the body, and Suneater bodies already run too hot. A male repeatedly given a powerful female's venom may gain status, protection, and nearness to power, but he lives under the strain of it. The more she bites him, the more his body chemistry shifts to match her own.

## Society

### The Matriarch

One Matriarch rules all the Suneater clans. The Kenivar-Jho are the central clan, and lesser clans exist beyond it, each with its own name, grievances, blood customs, and local powers.

To her people the Matriarch is more than a ruler. After thousands of years she has become something close to a living deity. She has outlasted clan histories, blood feuds, and most lineages that can be traced without records. Generations have been born, bred, culled, devoured, and ritually refined in her shadow, and many Suneaters can no longer tell the shape of their society from the shape of the Matriarch.

She controls breeding and access to power. She decides which bloodlines are encouraged and which are starved or sacrificed, and which are allowed to believe they are rising on their own strength. Every royal pregnancy is political. Daughters are born into the succession, and a valuable son never belongs wholly to himself.

Everyone expects one of her offspring to take her place eventually, and the Matriarch understands this better than anyone. She raises her daughters accordingly.

### Daughters of the Matriarch

A Matriarch who loves her daughter too softly makes a weak heir, and one who breaks her too thoroughly makes no heir at all. The balance is brutal and deliberate. Daughters grow up under pressure, denied enough comfort to sharpen them and given enough privilege to learn the taste of power. Every lesson cuts both ways, teaching them how to rule and how their mother will kill them if they reach too soon.

### Royal Lines

Royals have to project strength in everything: body, voice, posture, appetite, chosen mates, children, and enemies survived. Weakness in a royal invites challenge. Any sign of vulnerability starts speculation, a visible wound invites testing, and isolation sets rivals calculating. A royal who can't convince onlookers that devouring them would be costly has already started losing flesh in their minds.

Royal blood is desired literally as well as politically. The closer someone stands to the Matriarchal line, the more their body is worth after defeat. A royal Suneater's flesh carries concentrated bloodline, ritual refinement, and generations of cannibal inheritance, and eating it means taking in a fragment of the line's authority, or at least announcing that intention loudly enough that no one can pretend not to hear. A failed challenger can lose more than position and become the feast another rises on. Power alone isn't enough protection for a princess, prince, or high-blooded heir. They have to make the thought of eating them feel like ambition approaching suicide.

A royal who falls in private may still be shielded by secrecy. One who falls in public becomes an event, and the court gathers with mouths ready.

Royal children grow up knowing that admiration and appetite often wear the same face.

### Males

Within traditional Suneater society males are subservient to females, and only an outsider would mistake that for softness. They kneel because the hierarchy requires it, and they keep their pride while doing it.

A male lowered before a female sees himself as waiting. From beneath the line of her hand he watches and measures the room, ready for the command that gives his violence a direction. That is the ideal Suneaters raise their sons toward, ferocity held under discipline and pride under ownership, with arrogance sharpened into service. A powerful female has no use for a male who can't kneel, and less use for one who kneels without teeth.

From youth, males are raised as guardians, hunters, warriors, and providers, their bodies trained for endurance, pursuit, violence, and the labor of survival. They gather, kill, and defend what they must, and they stand between the female line and anything foolish enough to threaten it. They are valued for obedience and for the quality of their violence once obedience lets it loose.

Male loyalty has to be earned. A female secures it through power, blood, protection, provision, venom, victory, or a lineage strong enough to make refusal look foolish. Once won, it can become frighteningly absolute. A claimed male may bare his throat to the woman who holds him and tear out the throat of anyone who mistakes that for weakness.

Royal males hold a more complicated place. They kneel before royal women, since the Matriarchal line is the axis their world turns on. Before lesser Suneaters, especially those from weak lines, they may show no restraint at all, and a royal male may treat a lesser warrior as sport, an obstacle, or meat temporarily shaped like a person. His subservience points upward only.

Outsiders often miss this. A male Suneater may lower his eyes before a princess and still have pride enough to start a clan war over an insult from anyone else. He may follow a female's command while believing himself above every lesser-blooded body in the room. Suneater society leaves male arrogance intact, leashes it to female power, and calls the leash civilization.

## Cannibalism

### The Devouring of Kin

Suneaters eat each other.

Hunger started the practice. The Dune Sea doesn't reliably feed anyone who lives by ordinary expectations, and Suneaters long ago gave up the idea that all bodies hold equal value. To them flesh is sustenance and blood is inheritance, and the hearts carry power. Eating another Suneater means surviving them and also claiming what remains of their strength before sand, rot, or lesser mouths can take it.

Kin is preferred because kin is compatible, and the closer the blood, the cleaner the absorption. Royal flesh is wanted most of all for the strength of the line it carries (see Royal Lines). Suneaters eat when they starve, but they also eat because their society holds that strength should never be allowed to leave the blood.

Even the compassionate grow up inside this reasoning, and refusing is more complicated than disgust. In a famine, a dead body may mean living children. After a battle, a defeated enemy's hearts may steady a warrior who would otherwise collapse. In royal politics, leaving a body untouched can read as insult, weakness, or treason. Mercy among Suneaters is rarely clean enough to stay innocent.

### Hearts of the Defeated

Eating the hearts of someone defeated in battle is expected. Orthodox belief holds that leaving them untouched after a worthy fight denies the meaning of victory.

The rite varies by rank. Among lesser warriors it may be immediate and bloody, witnessed by survivors. Among nobles it becomes formal, with the heart removed and presented according to hierarchy. In royal conflicts the right to consume may be reserved, granted, or withheld as a political act.

After larger clan skirmishes the Matriarch may visit the aftermath herself and claim the bodies of her own. Outsiders mistake it for mourning, but she comes to take inventory and pass judgment, and the act is sacramental as well. The most loyal may receive portions of the dead to strengthen themselves. The gift shows that the Matriarch has seen the battlefield, weighed the bodies, and decided who may rise from the losses. Being denied the dead is a punishment in itself.

## Death

### The Unconsumed Dead

Among Suneaters the dead can't be ignored. A Suneater body that isn't devoured, burned, or ritually contained may rise. The corrupted Weave used throughout life to shape flesh, purge impurities, and hold the bloodline's improbable stability together doesn't always release the body cleanly at death. What remains becomes vessel, meat, and doorway, and the Ancestors come through it.

The risen are feral at first, driven by hunger before identity and motion before memory. They feel pain in no useful way and never tire as living bodies do, and they move on corrupted pattern, old blood, and need.

Most Suneaters believe an Ancestor can gain sentience inside the corpse by eating the flesh of the living. Whether that is literal possession, corrupted memory, or the Weave shaping a dead brain around an ancestral imprint is a question for scholars who aren't being chased across the dunes. Suneaters care about the practical truth. A ghoul that feeds grows more dangerous, and one that feeds enough may begin to remember.

### Ghouls

Burning feral risen to ash is common practice, done for hygiene with no ceremony attached.

An intact ghoul threatens the whole territory. It hunts whatever life it can find, drawn first to warmth, then to blood, then to the stronger resonance of Lyrian flesh. Broken limbs drag until the body finds a new way to move, and burned flesh keeps coming until the animating pattern collapses or the body is ash. Once it has fed, it may begin to anticipate, take cover, recognize clan markings, and speak words from the body's former life, or from something older using its tongue. At that stage Suneaters stop calling it a ghoul and call it a failure.

In a land already ruled by hunger, the unconsumed dead mock the living, all appetite and violence with none of the hierarchy, pride, or doctrine that gives Suneater hunger its shape.

Suneaters seldom admit fear, but ghouls are an exception they carefully rename as disgust. A risen body proves that even death may not free someone from the bloodline. It also proves that the rituals behind Suneater strength carry consequences no Matriarch can fully command.

The compassionate burn their dead quickly and cleanly when they can, while the orthodox devour, render, or assign the corpse according to status. The desperate do whatever keeps the body from standing up again.

### Ancestors

Suneaters venerate their Ancestors and fear them in the same breath. The dead are honored, consumed, invoked, and contained, and never trusted.

The Ancestors stand for the continuity of blood, and also for the danger of blood that refuses to end. Priestly doctrine casts them as witnesses to strength, approving those who devour and abandoning those too weak to take what survival requires. Older, quieter traditions treat them more warily, as remnants instead of guides, hungers that learned names.

A sentient Ancestor in a risen body is a nightmare and an omen. Some clans have tried to bind them, consult them, or turn them loose on enemies. Most of those stories end on the same lesson: hunger does not become loyal because it remembers who it used to be.

## Matter-Wave Corruption

Suneaters use the word corruption differently from outsiders. To foreign scholars it means damage, instability in the Weave, degraded flesh, and natural law contaminated. To Suneaters it means refinement through pressure. Matter and Weave were never meant to stay clean. Purity is brittle and dies first, and whatever adapts to strain without breaking is what survives.

That philosophy sits at the center of almost every Suneater practice. Venom alters the body through corruptive imprinting, and cannibal inheritance folds foreign strength into living blood. Ardor burns away restraint and lays instinct bare. Even the royal breeding programs are built on controlled instability, carefully managed concentrations of traits that would destroy weaker lineages outright.

The danger is that corruption can't tell improvement from ruin. A body shaped too aggressively may lose cohesion, its flesh living on while the identity inside it fractures. Some become visibly monstrous, with warped bone growth, uncontrolled Ardor, unstable venom, collapsing senses, or Weave saturation severe enough to distort the reality around them. Others fail more quietly as obsession replaces judgment and hunger replaces loyalty, until the person survives physically but can no longer live within any structure except appetite.

The Matriarchal lines have lasted because they have spent thousands of years learning how much corruption a bloodline can bear before it consumes itself. Even so, every royal birth is a gamble.
