# LYRIA
## Luminari Imperial Intelligence Directorate
### Institutional Reference Document

---

## Overview

The Luminari Imperial Intelligence Directorate is the Empire's primary intelligence service. It collects, analyzes, and acts on information bearing on Imperial security, answers to the Imperial Council, and is split into three divisions. Each has its own mandate and its own culture, and each keeps up a steady low-grade rivalry with the other two. The Empire built it that way on purpose.

A single intelligence service gathers power quickly and gets hard to oversee. Three competing services check one another. Each will surface what another might bury for its own convenience, and no one division's blind spots go unchallenged for long. The price is friction, duplicated effort, and the occasional jurisdictional standoff that stalls everything. The Empire pays it willingly. The divisions have their own views on the arrangement and argue them mainly through the budget process.

Other bodies gather intelligence too. Military intelligence, corporate security, and planetary authorities each collect within their own domains. The Directorate's mandate is broader, covering anything that crosses jurisdictional lines or grows beyond the structures meant to contain it, and it reports directly to the Council. Its assessments carry more weight in Imperial decisions than any other source.

---

## The Director

On paper, the Director holds the most powerful post in Imperial intelligence. The real job is narrower and odder.

The Director does not run operations. The three division heads do, and they defend that autonomy fiercely. What the Director controls is the flow of information between the divisions and the Council. Council attention decides funding and mandate, so that control goes a long way. A capable Director seldom has to give an order. They decide which intelligence reaches the Council, in what sequence, with what framing, and alongside what context. A Veil Directorate assessment of Protectorate weapons development reads one way if it lands before an Office of Oversight report on Moonweaver crystal irregularities and another way if it lands after. The Director sets the order and lets the Council reach conclusions it believes it reached alone.

The Director also settles disputes the division heads cannot settle among themselves: a jurisdictional conflict that has stalled, a fight over an asset that has started to cause operational damage, two divisions working the same problem toward opposite ends. The Director's rulings are binding, and Directors make them sparingly, since every major ruling leaves at least one division head unhappy. Division heads keep score. A lost ruling gets filed away and brought out again at budget time, during the next dispute over an asset, or whenever the Director next needs a favor.

Much of the job, then, is managing three organizations that each think they ought to be running the whole thing. A Director has to rule often enough to stay useful and evenly enough that no division feels singled out. The ones who last tend to look idle while doing a great deal.

The Director's steadiest advantage is that no one else sees both what the Council wants and what the divisions are doing. A Council vote cannot take that away, and a division head who courts Council members directly cannot wear it down.

Every Director knows that all three division heads believe they could do the job better, and that at least one of them is usually right. The Director finds this motivating.

## The Veil Directorate
### Division One: External Threats

The Veil Directorate believes it is the reason the Empire has not yet lost the cold war. Whether that is true depends on how one defines losing.

Its mandate covers every strategic threat beyond the Empire's borders. The Galactic Protectorate takes up perhaps eighty percent of its attention at any given time. The remainder goes to independent powers, alien species whose intentions are unclear, and Tear events that could tip the galactic balance before either major power is ready.

#### Methodology

The division relies on deep-cover insertion, slow cultivation of assets, and signals intelligence drawn from the crystal communications network the Empire maintains across the galaxy. Its operatives spend years building lives inside Protectorate territory and working their way into positions where they can observe without drawing notice. Many do not come back, a figure that rarely comes up in budget meetings.

Protectorate counter-intelligence is the division's central problem. The Protectorate built its ideology on rejecting Majik and elevating enhanced human capability, and its security personnel show it. They are physically formidable and mentally disciplined, and they are trained specifically to spot Luminari methods. The Veil Directorate has lost more assets to them than to anything else. Over the years those losses have pushed it away from inserting its own people and toward technical collection, along with the recruitment of people already embedded in Protectorate structures for reasons of their own.

The division has also been lobbying, without much fanfare, to make formal use of Iriah navigators. Both powers value the Iriah for their neutrality, which makes them ideal couriers of information, and the Veil Directorate wants a standing arrangement. The Stellar Enforcement Agency already works with several Iriah flotillas, considers this poaching, and has said so at length.

#### Relationship with Majik

No division is more conflicted about Majik than the Veil Directorate, and its reasons are practical. It works mainly in Protectorate space, and the Protectorate hunts shapers. Protectorate security is trained to read the physical tells of active shaping and carries detection gear that the enhancement programs refined for exactly that purpose. A shaper sent into that territory runs risks a conventional operative never faces.

The division handles this with two tracks. Conventional operatives, who carry no Weave presence, take the Protectorate assignments. Shapers stay almost entirely out of Protectorate space and work closer to home, in Tear-adjacent regions, Lyrian space, and the outer colonies, where the Weave is tangled and Majik is common enough that a shaper goes unnoticed.

The arrangement causes lasting resentment. Judged purely by what they can gather, the division's shapers are often its best people, because attunement lets them perceive things conventional operatives miss. Keeping them away from the main theatre strikes the shapers as waste. Colleagues who have watched shapers get burned by Protectorate counter-intelligence call it ordinary caution. The argument has never been settled, and it runs through the division at every level.

#### Internal Culture

Veil Directorate operatives carry a professional arrogance. They handle the Empire's existential threats and are well aware of it. They see the Office of Oversight as paranoid clerks who have confused watching allies with defending the Empire, and the Stellar Enforcement Agency as border police with an inflated sense of importance. They mean it, and they say it with the condescension of people certain that theirs is the only work that counts.

## The Office of Imperial Oversight
### Division Two: Internal Threats

The Office of Imperial Oversight does not advertise what it does.

Its founding charter speaks of the integrity of Imperial partnerships and the internal cohesion of the Empire's constituent factions. In practice it watches everyone inside the borders who isn't an ordinary citizen, along with a good number who are. The Moonweavers are its main focus and its main frustration.

#### Methodology

The Office prefers patience and indirection. It cultivates people already inside sensitive factions and lets information travel through existing channels, where a new channel might be noticed. Its analysts are the Directorate's best at assembling a plausible picture from fragments. The field work draws little attention, but the institutional memory goes further back than either other division's, and the files hold the kind of background that keeps old intelligence usable.

The Moonweaver file is its most sensitive and least resolved. The synthetic crystal program, unknown to anyone else in the galaxy, sits in the Office's most restricted archive. Beside it is a decade of analysis on the program's strategic meaning, the diplomatic cost if it were exposed, and what the Empire should do if the Moonweavers ever decided their interests no longer matched the Empire's. None of that analysis has produced a conclusion the Office is willing to act on. Every attempt to see further into Moonweaver society comes back with nothing, or with something that raises more questions than it answers. The Office has learned to live with the uncertainty the way one lives with a crack in a load-bearing wall.

#### Relationship with Majik

The Office distrusts Majik on evidence. It has spent decades watching what happens when a shaper with deep knowledge and wide access begins to come apart. The slide into Psychosis is rarely dramatic. It starts as eccentricity and turns into obsession, and by the time anyone gives it a name, the damage is done and can't be fully measured, because the person who did it was the one trusted with the sensitive files.

As a result, the Office treats shapers the way a careful engineer treats a volatile compound. It keeps them contained and never lets anything important rest on them. Its shaper assets work in narrow lanes. A specialist brought in to read Moonweaver Weave signatures reads Weave signatures and nothing else. They never see the larger picture their work feeds into or learn what question it answers. Their assessment goes to a conventional handler, who folds it into an analysis the shaper will never see.

Reaching any post with administrative authority requires a clean non-shaper background. No rule says so, and none is needed. A reliable shaper might rise to senior field agent but will not become a section head.

That outlook has led the Office somewhere uncomfortable. Its own risk assessments have arrived at conclusions close to Protectorate doctrine on the dangers of Majik. The Office has no Protectorate sympathies in any political sense, and its people are thoroughly Imperial. Still, in certain offices, people have started asking whether the Protectorate might be wrong about the right response while being correct about the risk. The Office has not decided whether to send that question upward or let it develop on its own.

The Office also runs the Directorate's psychological assessment program for shapers in all three divisions. The program reaches well beyond the Office's own staff and gives it considerable power, as described under Oversight and the Internal Mandate.

#### Internal Culture

Oversight analysts tend toward a type. They are methodical and skeptical, at ease with ambiguity, and their long institutional memory makes them slow to trust new information and slower to trust new people. Talking with one feels faintly like being evaluated, even when nothing important is under discussion. They know how the other divisions see them. They think the description is fair and see no reason to change.

## The Stellar Enforcement Agency
### Division Three: Enforcement

The Stellar Enforcement Agency is the Directorate's operational arm within Imperial borders. When the other two divisions call it that, they don't mean it kindly, and the Agency knows.

Its mandate is active threats: piracy, smuggling, unauthorized Tear research, rogue shapers working outside sanctioned frameworks, the illegal crystal trade, organized crime with political cover, and the wide range of problems too big for a planetary authority and too small for the military. It is the busiest division and the least prestigious, and the pace of its work often leaves little room for strategic thinking.

#### Operational Model

The Agency operates in three modes, and its field commanders put a lot of judgment into choosing between them.

Coordination is the most common mode and the least glamorous. When a regional problem surfaces, an Agency team goes to the nearest authority with jurisdiction, and that authority suddenly finds itself under temporary Agency command, its resources redirected to an operation it didn't plan and may not fully understand. Local pilots fly Agency operation plans and local enforcement makes arrests under Agency authority, while local intelligence assets are briefed only as far as they need to be. The model works when the local authority is competent and honest. It works less well in the fringe systems, where authorities are underfunded, sometimes corrupt, and occasionally part of the problem.

Direct intervention is the Agency's public face. Its teams are highly trained and carry the best equipment the Empire fields. They can operate in hostile Weave environments and are drilled to face shaper resistance, and they have the experience to handle organized piracy, rogue Tear research, or the delicate matter of an Imperial citizen whose political connections don't excuse what they've done.

Overwhelming force is rare and thoroughly documented. The Agency keeps planning capacity well beyond its own resources, including a standing library of military operation plans for threats that outgrow enforcement. A piracy network might swell into a military problem, a rogue shaper might gather enough followers to hold territory, or a Tear event might produce something no enforcement team can contain. In those cases the Agency pulls the matching plan and hands it to the military along with the intelligence package that makes it usable. The military gets clear direction, and the Agency gets the credit.

#### The Geography Problem

The Empire is vast, and the Agency's standing resources are not. Coordination exists because the alternative, an Agency large enough to act alone everywhere, is unaffordable and impossible besides. In theory its mandate covers everything inside the borders. In practice it triages constantly.

The outer systems are a permanent blind spot. They cover too much space to patrol, local authorities there are seldom dependable, and the Agency's resources are concentrated far away in the core worlds. The Iriah have understood this geography for generations. Their favored routes line up almost exactly with the areas where Agency response is slowest and local capacity weakest, and they chose them for that reason.

The Agency's authority to requisition local assets comes from Imperial charter, and nearly everyone subject to it resents it. Alongside its operational work, the Agency has had to learn the long, patient upkeep of local relationships. Regional liaisons spend most of their time keeping those relationships in good repair. Favors get done well ahead of when they'll be called in, and intelligence is shared generously enough to prove its worth and leave the recipient feeling obliged. The Agency is better at this than the other divisions give it credit for, mostly because neither of them has to do it on anything like the same scale.

#### Relationship with Majik

The Agency uses Majik more openly than either other division and thinks about it in the abstract least. Its shapers work cases alongside conventional agents, and their attunement is treated as one tool among several. Because Agency teams hunt rogue shapers, they have to be able to counter active shaping in the field, so Agency shapers train for field operations. The Agency runs the Directorate's only active counter-shaping program, which both other divisions have drawn on without ever acknowledging it.

Psychosis cases cluster in the outer systems, where response is slowest and local capacity thinnest, and interdicting them is one of the Agency's most exhausting recurring jobs. A shaper who loses coherence while still holding operational knowledge is a threat at large, possibly still shaping, somewhere the Agency can't reach in time.

#### Internal Culture

Agency people are pragmatic, and the other divisions tend to mistake that for a lack of sophistication. They have learned many times over that the elegant solution and the workable one often differ, and they pick the workable one. They improvise more readily than the other divisions and have little patience for analysis that never turns into action.

Careers here vary more than elsewhere in the Directorate. The strongest Agency hands can work in any operational mode. The Agency counts this as a strength. The other divisions call it a lack of focus, which the Agency considers a fair summary of the difference between people who solve problems and people who study them.

## Interdivisional Dynamics

The three divisions overlap by design, and the friction is intended.

### Veil Directorate and Office of Oversight

This pairing carries the most ideological charge. The Veil Directorate resents the Office's authority over shaper assessment, which puts its shapers under scrutiny its conventional operatives never face and splits the division into two tiers. It can't fix that without the Director's help, and the Director has consistently declined to give it. Beneath that sits the Office's drift toward Protectorate-like conclusions about Majik, which the Veil Directorate reads as the early stages of institutional capture, if not the finished article. Neither grievance has been formally filed. Both color every interaction below the formal level.

### Office of Oversight and Stellar Enforcement Agency

These two get along best, and their relationship is also the most complicated. They run informal information-sharing arrangements that no Director has ever approved or shut down. Enforcement operations turn up intelligence that belongs in Oversight files, and Oversight analysis flags targets before the Agency has found them. Both sides benefit, so the arrangement holds. It stays unofficial because putting it in writing would force each to acknowledge the other's jurisdiction in terms neither will accept. Where they do clash is over how the Agency uses its shapers. The Agency's pragmatism produces exactly the uncontrolled situations the Office's whole structure exists to prevent, and the two have been arguing about it for about as long as the three-division structure has existed.

### Veil Directorate and Stellar Enforcement Agency

This is the most openly hostile pairing. The Veil Directorate's pursuit of Iriah navigators cuts across enforcement relationships the Agency has spent years building. Once an Iriah operator has been approached for recruitment, prosecuting them gets much harder, and the Veil Directorate usually makes the approach without telling the Agency first. The Agency has complained to the Director several times, and the Director has acknowledged the complaint each time. The recruiting continues.

## The Internal Economy of Information

Formal channels for sharing intelligence between divisions exist and are documented, and the divisions use them as little as possible. A formal request leaves a record and has to be justified. It invites a request in return and signals interest in a subject the requesting division would usually prefer to keep quiet. It also creates a debt. Grant a formal request and the other division will expect repayment, and debts between divisions are remembered precisely.

Most intelligence moves informally instead, through relationships that either predate the rivalry or ignore it. Analysts who trained together before being assigned to competing divisions stay in touch for their entire careers. An operative who once served alongside someone now in another division trusts that person in ways the org chart doesn't account for. These relationships carry most of the traffic, and they run on favors. Someone passes along a piece of information before it's asked for, knowing it will count later, or answers a question nobody officially asked. A small favor now is understood to buy help later, when it's needed.

This informal economy moves faster than any approval process, and it lets the divisions work as a single intelligence service in practice while staying three competing ones on paper. The Director knows about it and depends on it. A Directorate whose divisions shared only what they were required to share would grind to a halt.

Nobody can fully control it, either. The division heads know it exists and rely on it, but most of it is invisible to them, so they can never be sure their own people aren't trading away something they'd rather keep. A cross-divisional relationship brings information in and carries information out, often at the same time. The heads tolerate this because shutting down informal contact would cost more than it saved.

The people who do best in this economy are seldom the most senior. They are the ones with the widest networks and the best sense of what to trade and what to keep. A mid-level analyst whose old classmates are now spread across all three divisions can learn more than a section head, because they hear things no formal request would turn up. People like that know their value, and some have turned it into protection and latitude well beyond what their rank would give them.

The system has limits. A favor traded badly, whether the information turns out wrong or exposes whoever passed it along, damages the relationship it came through, and those relationships take a long time to rebuild. Anyone who trades dishonestly finds their contacts drying up as the network routes around them.

## Oversight and the Internal Mandate

The Office of Imperial Oversight watches internal threats, and on the most literal reading, the Veil Directorate and the Stellar Enforcement Agency are internal. No one in the Directorate says this out loud.

The Office's charter neither authorizes nor forbids monitoring its sibling divisions. It refers to the internal cohesion of the Empire and the integrity of its institutions, and a motivated reader could stretch that language to cover the intelligence apparatus itself. The Office has never argued that reading openly and has never had to. It has the capability and the inclination either way, and both other divisions assume it watches them as closely as it can.

They have good reason to. The shaper assessment program functions as internal affairs. Because the Office conducts psychological evaluations of shapers in all three divisions, its files document the vulnerabilities of the other divisions' most sensitive people. When a Veil Directorate shaper begins showing early signs of Psychosis, the Office knows before the Veil Directorate does. That gives it leverage over a sibling division, and everyone involved understands this while keeping up the fiction that it is ordinary personnel management. A deteriorating operative who is still useful may be monitored and left unflagged, so the Office can get full value from them while it narrows their access a little at a time.

No division can say with confidence how far the watching goes. The Veil Directorate assumes the worst and runs its most sensitive operations with counter-surveillance aimed at the Office as much as at the Protectorate. The Agency has less delicate tradecraft to protect. It assumes it is being watched and mostly shrugs, reasoning that its operations are too numerous and fast-moving to track in any useful way. Either division could be wrong, in either direction.

The Director leaves the question open on purpose. Formally authorizing the Office to watch the other divisions would cause a crisis the moment it came out, and it would come out. Forbidding it would cut the Director off from information the job depends on. The Office does what its mandate can be read to allow and never presses the question of whether it is permitted, while the Director keeps the option of disowning it.

The other two divisions protect themselves the only way available, by treating the Office as a hostile service wherever their most sensitive material is concerned. That erodes the cooperation the Directorate is supposed to run on, and given the structure, it is entirely rational. The Office finds the suspicion insulting and accurate in equal measure, and has accepted the contradiction the way it accepts every other awkward corner of its mandate.

## The Directorate in the Wider World

The Directorate's existence is public by choice. A service no one knows about can't deter anyone or be held up as a threat, and it can't claim credit when credit would help the Empire. Its structure, methods, and the specifics of what it knows stay secret, and each division has a very different public reputation.

### The Stellar Enforcement Agency

The Agency is the most visible division and the best liked. Most citizens see it as whoever shows up when a threat gets too big for everyone else, and that reputation is mostly deserved. The Agency doesn't spy on citizens in any way they would notice. Its work inside the Empire is done alongside local authorities against real threats. The resentment it stirs up comes from institutions, from authorities who find an Agency team taking over a situation they thought was theirs. Ordinary citizens see only the threat dealt with, and they regard the Agency with the plain approval people give to anyone who runs toward danger on their behalf.

### The Office of Imperial Oversight

Everyone knows the Office and almost no one understands it. Its public face is mundane and everywhere. It enforces tax compliance, administers regulations, runs customs on stations and worlds, and handles the vast, dull work of making sure Imperial rules are followed. Citizens deal with it over permits, tariffs, and audits, and it does that work thoroughly. It also makes excellent cover. An organization entitled to inspect cargo and audit accounts, with offices on every significant world, has access that a conventional intelligence service would have to steal, and it has that access openly, as routine. Only the upper levels of local government know or suspect that the Office is an intelligence service at all. To everyone else it is the taxman and the customs inspector, too ordinary to notice.

### The Veil Directorate

The public knows least about the Veil Directorate and almost never sees it. Its work happens beyond the borders, and most people know only that it exists and occasionally scores a success described in vague terms. Inside the Empire it has a small footprint, limited to intelligence gathering and counter-espionage aimed at the Protectorate. The citizens it protects will almost never learn anything specific it has done. A division built to work unseen in hostile territory has no use for a public profile and makes no effort to have one.

### The Imperial Council

The Council considers the Directorate indispensable and a little dangerous. Its members value the access and know that what reaches them has been shaped along the way. The sharper ones understand that the Director's framing is a form of power, and they try to form their own picture of the divisions by cultivating the division heads directly. The Director knows this and manages them accordingly.

### The Imperial Military

The military's professional rivalry with the Directorate swings between cooperation and open friction. It particularly resents the Agency's overwhelming-force capability. To senior officers, standing operation plans that let an intelligence division direct military assets look like a civilian agency reaching into their territory. The two work together most smoothly against the Protectorate, where the Veil Directorate and the military both understand that the cold war leaves them no choice.

### The Moonweavers

The Moonweavers know they are watched. Their whole position depends on controlling what others know about them, and they are not foolish enough to assume otherwise. They manage their relationship with the Empire on the assumption of surveillance, revealing what helps them and hiding what doesn't, with enough skill that the synthetic crystal program has stayed secret through years of close attention from the Office. They find the attention more flattering than worrying, since it confirms how much they matter.

### The Galactic Protectorate

The Protectorate considers the Directorate its main intelligence adversary and the Veil Directorate its direct counterpart. The two know each other as longtime opponents do, through years of operations against one another, assets turned and lost, methods observed and countered. The Protectorate respects the Veil Directorate's reach. It also sees the division's reliance on shapers as a weakness to exploit and as proof of everything it believes about the Empire's reckless tolerance for Majik. Services that have studied each other across generations develop an odd closeness, and these two have it.

### Independent Operators, Smugglers, and Criminals

To people working outside the law, the Agency mostly stands in for the whole Directorate. It is the part of Imperial intelligence they meet, the part that arrives once something has grown large enough to notice. They know it as competent and persistent. It picks targets by priorities that aren't always clear and leaves alone plenty that local authorities would pursue. The smarter ones know that attracting Agency attention changes their situation entirely, and they keep their operations below the level that would trigger it. The Iriah flotillas are better at this than anyone.

## A Note on the Bael File

The file on Baelindover Corsibaven Praetoris, reference PRE-7741-BC, belongs to the Agency. The Corsivane interdiction falls under Agency jurisdiction, and the Agency produced the surveillance and port documentation.

The Office of Oversight holds restricted material on the Thresh Incident. The Agency formally requested it and was formally refused, on the grounds that it touches active monitoring operations. The Agency calls the refusal obstruction of an open enforcement case. The Director has not ruled.

The Veil Directorate, meanwhile, has opened its own assessment of the subject, drawn by the navigational profile and the depth of the Moonweaver connection. It has not told the Agency. The Agency suspects as much and is trying to confirm it. The Office knows about both files and has said nothing to either division.

For the Luminari Imperial Intelligence Directorate, this is a fairly ordinary day.
