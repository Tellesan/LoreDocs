# ERDANA

### A Setting Guide for a Dying World

---

## The Short Version

The world is called Erdana. So is the goddess of life the faithful pray to, though almost nobody knows the two are the same thing.

Erdana is dying. It's happening slowly, the way a big fire burns down. She has centuries left, each a little dimmer than the one before. The life that fills the world, the ember, is leaking out of it, faster every time someone uses it. Whole regions have already gone dark and cold, drained into gray waste that sucks the life out of anything trying to cross it.

On its own, that wouldn't drive much of a story. Centuries is longer than anyone alive will see. Nobody starts a rebellion over a sunset.

The trouble is on the throne. For two hundred years the Emperor has been drinking from Erdana directly, at her heart. He calls it just rule and the Church calls it Erdana's blessing. What he's doing is killing her. He's cut millennia of slow decline down to a couple of centuries so that he can live forever while the world foots the bill.

The setting is built on a beautiful lie and on a slandered remnant who are guilty of nearly everything except killing the world. Hanging over both is a war that ends everything if anybody wins it the obvious way.

---

## How the Magic Works

### Ember

**Ember** is the life of the world. It's in the soil and the air and the green, but mostly it's inside living things, held there the way fruit holds water. Every living creature is a small, full reservoir of ember on legs. Whatever isn't sealed inside something is loose in the open world, where there's a little less of it every year.

The common folk and the Vigil call it **ember**. It's a plain word with no ceremony to it, the life in things, as in "the ember's gone out of this ground."

The Church calls it **Grace**. Priests don't talk about spending ember. They channel Grace.

The old Vigil scholars called it the **Wellspring**, a source you could draw from forever. The few of them still alive mostly won't say the word anymore.

### Gleam

You can't see ember itself. What you can see, if you have the sight for it, is **gleam**, the faint light ember gives off. To those eyes the living green burns while the dying edges flicker low and the deadzones show nothing at all. A skilled sensitive reads the whole world this way, seeing where the light pools thick, where it's thin, and where it's gone. Someone stuffed with stolen ember blazes like a bonfire, while a spent old master barely shows. A corpse in the grays is black.

### Who Can Touch It

Anyone born able to feel and move ember is **Kindled**, whether or not anyone ever teaches them what to do with it. Most never get taught. They hide what they are in a world that hunts them, fumble along on their own, or go their whole lives without a name for what's strange about them.

What happens to a Kindled kid depends on who finds them first. If it's the Vigil, they're trained as **Tenders** and taught to spend themselves carefully and carry their dead. If it's the Church, they become **Kindling** and get burned as fuel for holier purposes. Most are found by nobody and live out their lives keeping their heads down.

### The Cost, and the Ladder

Using ember always costs ember. There are three ways to pay, stacked like rungs on a ladder. Each rung is harder to reach than the last. How far up someone has climbed says a lot about whether they're a Tender or a monster.

The bottom rung is spending yourself. You burn your own ember to power the work. Anyone Kindled can do it from day one, though it wrecks you. You age wrong and hollow out, getting quieter and grayer and stranger until there's almost nothing left. The kindest, most selfless people tend to end up here. It takes years to kill them.

The middle rung is taking from other people, skimming ember off the living around you or out of someone who just died. It costs you nothing and it's far stronger. Not everyone can manage it; you have to work your way up to it. It's also the line. Cross it and you're **Turned**. Skimming like this leaves people gray and tired without emptying them. Emptying a person outright is the Witherer's art.

The top rung is drinking from the world itself, pulling straight out of the open, dying land. Almost nobody gets there. It feels harmless, since nobody drops dead and the supply looks endless. The supply is Erdana, though. Every sip brings her end a little closer.

Each step up makes you stronger by putting the cost on someone else. The most powerful practitioners are the ones who've stopped paying their own way. The thing on the throne sits at the top of this same ladder. His magic is the ordinary kind pushed as far as it goes.

There's a quieter cost on top of all this, at every rung. You can't touch ember without touching the mind it belongs to, so every bit of magic lets a little of Erdana into you. Old masters of every discipline end up carrying a grief that isn't theirs. They go still and distant, half full of something huge and dying. The calm people admire in an old master comes from being mostly spent and mostly full of a god's sorrow.

## The Disciplines

You can't do anything with ember by reaching for it any old way. You have to narrow yourself down to one way of holding it, a particular grip. Doing that reshapes your own ember into a channel with a fixed shape. The first real attunement cuts a groove that you run along from then on. Cutting a second groove that's very different means fighting the first.

So practitioners specialize. Most master one discipline, or two if the shapes are similar and they learned young. Three is the stuff of legend. When it does happen, it usually means someone who's shallow in all three. A true master has reshaped themselves so completely into one tool that they couldn't learn another to save their life.

Disciplines are named after the person who practices them. How they're fueled is a separate question, since nearly any discipline can run on any rung of the ladder. That's how two people from the same school can end up a saint and a monster, depending on who bleeds for their work.

#### The Quickener

Quickening is the swordsman's art. You burn ember into your own body for impossible speed, strength, and reflexes. From the inside, the world thins out and slows down until a blade swings at you lazily, like it's underwater. From the outside there's no glow and no gesture, only someone moving too fast and too precisely. Fueled from yourself, it burns you out young and beautiful, with cold fingers by forty. A Turned Quickener moves like a god while everyone within thirty feet sags and goes gray, feeding his speed.

#### The Hand

The Hand moves things without touching them. It doesn't push. Everything has a thread of ember in it. The Hand grabs that thread and the object follows. Things come reluctantly, dragging, because you're hauling on the part of them that's the dying world. Heavy work leaves a drained chill in the air and in the object.

#### The Voice

The Voice works on the ember sealed inside another person. A light touch can suggest things, calm someone down, or ease or stoke their fear. A heavier hand can compel. At its deepest it's plain taking. It feels like reaching inside someone, past the skin, into a warm sealed thing they don't know they're carrying. People fear Voices for that. A lot of them end up strange and lonely, having lost the sense that other people are closed off from them at all.

#### The Mender

Mending runs on the cruelest math in the world. To close a wound you pour ember into it. That ember has to come from somewhere. A Mender who uses their own heals by giving themselves away, going grayer and older with every life they save. A Mender who takes from bystanders can save the dying man on the table by quietly draining the person holding his hand. A Mender who's learned to drink from the world heals with no visible cost at all. People love them for it and call them holy, while every healing pushes the god a little closer to death. The grip that pours ember into flesh, turned around, pulls it out.

#### The Witherer

Withering is that grip turned around, the destroyer's art. Where any Turned can skim ember off the people near them, a Witherer reaches into something alive and pulls its ember out by hand until there's nothing left. It takes contact and it takes time, because the body fights to hold on to its own life, so a Witherer kills the way someone drowns a person, by getting close and holding on through the struggle. A brush leaves the victim grayed and aged and missing a piece of themselves. Emptying someone completely is slow, committed work that floods the killer with the terror and grief of the death they're causing. That residue piles up over a career and drives Witherers strange. Someone strong in their own ember is harder to take, which makes a Witherer an apocalypse against the defenseless and a tough duel against a master.

Cutting this groove into a child on purpose is ugly work that almost nobody will do, so trained Witherers are rare. Nearly all of them come from Turned lineages breeding their own. The Church does the same thing deliberately, but only ever inside its Sentinels (more on them below). The gentlest Menders get watched for the day they flip the grip, since the two disciplines share a channel and only the direction is different.

#### The Shaper

A Shaper works on ember itself to change what something is. Pull the ember out fast as heat and you get fire. Loosen whatever holds a metal in shape and it runs and re-sets. It's the art of transformation, for making and for destroying. Shapers are also the only people who can make **soulsteel**, which puts them at the center of the Vigil.

#### The Listener

Listeners are the seers. A Listener opens up to the ember and lets it talk. What talks is Erdana, since the ember is Erdana. What comes through is the grief and terror and slow dying of an enormous mind that has no language. The Listener comes back with fragments, dread, and certainties they can't explain. Deep Listeners go blind in a strange inward way and start talking in the rhythm of whatever they've been hearing. None of them realize it's one particular voice, or that it's begging.

#### The Tide-reader

A Tide-reader feels ember moving across the world, where it flows and pools and drains, the way you'd feel currents in dark water against your skin. It's a modest, respectable skill. Tide-readers find good living land, pick safe paths through deadzones, and know which way the green is shrinking before the farmers do. Everyone knows the land is greenest near Abira. The faith calls that holiness. A Tide-reader patient enough and well-traveled enough to compare the flow in a dozen far-apart places could feel something nobody can see, the ember itself running inward, everywhere, toward one point like water toward a drain. That point is the holiest site in the world.

> #### Pairings
>
> Disciplines group by the kind of grip. The Quickener and the Hand reach outward, making them the warrior's pair. The Voice and the Mender reach into the life of other people, which is why the Vigil always kept a nervous eye on them. The Listener and the Tide-reader are the receptive ones. Someone who had both could feel the inward lean and also faintly hear what's crying at the center of it, which makes them the person most likely to stumble onto the truth. People like that exist, but you could go a lifetime without meeting one.

## Soulsteel and the Vessels

**Soulsteel** is steel a Shaper has worked so it can hold ember. Normal metal can't. Pour ember into it and it runs out like water into sand, or worse, it drains whoever's pouring. Anything made from soulsteel is a **vessel**. Vessels get named for what they're for.

Sitting idle, soulsteel looks like a fine, bright metal close to chrome. You can treat and color it like any other steel. The ember inside it shows as gleam. A Tide-reader can see a small charge and any Kindled can see a bigger one. A full vessel working hard puts out light anybody can see. A drawn vigil-blade lights up its own edge; a Sentinel at full burn glows like a forge. You can tell how much charge is left by how brightly it shines.

Rare specialists make soulsteel by hand, slowly, one vessel at a time. It's never been common anywhere. Even in the Vigil's best years nobody was mass-producing it. There were enough Shapers working openly that a Tender could expect a blade and a cell could expect its vessels. That was all. Those days are gone. The craft is down to a few hunted Shapers who can't reliably supply even the Vigil. A vigil-blade used to be something every Tender could count on. Now it's an heirloom people guard because there might never be another one. A cell with a Shaper has a future. A cell without one is living off what it already has. The Church calls all of it witch-steel and hunts Shapers harder than any other Kindled, because killing the makers ends the craft without having to catch everyone carrying it.

### The Charge

Vessels don't leak. Ember stays put until it's spent, which is why somebody can pull an ancient vessel out of a forgotten crack still glowing after centuries, or dig up a buried vigil-blade with its long-dead master still sealed inside.

How fast a vessel fills depends on how fine its work is. A vigil-blade has to cut at a hair's width and respond instantly, so its ember has to be set in carefully. Charging one takes an hour or two of quiet, meditative channeling. A crossing-core only has to scatter ember into the air around it, so you can pour charge in as fast as you like without it caring how the ember lands. Spending is always faster than filling. A full vessel holds hours of patient work that can be gone in seconds, which is why a full one is precious and an empty one is a real loss.

Fine charging takes control and focus, so you can't fill a fine vessel by violently ripping ember out of someone, no matter how good the ember is. Nobody does delicate work while tearing fuel out of a screaming victim. A blade needs the slow, calm method whatever the source. Crude vessels will take anything, from anywhere, fast.

A Tender charges their own vessel from their own ember, quietly. After a hard fight, a Tender sits down with the blade and slowly pours themselves back into it. A Tender too drained to spare anything can't refill it at all, which leaves them with an empty blade and nothing left in themselves either.

You can charge someone else's vessel, but it's hard going at first, since you're working against the structure its maker built in. It gets easier. As you charge and use a borrowed blade, you leave your own ember in it and learn how it was made, until enough of you is in there that it answers like it's yours. That's how an inherited blade becomes the heir's. It's also why a stolen blade stays hostile to a thief who doesn't have the years and the skill to learn it.

Ember can come from your own life, from another living person drained into the vessel, from someone who just died before their ember has had time to scatter, or, for the rare Kindled who can manage it, from the world itself, though that's slower and harder than channeling living ember. Ember is ember wherever it comes from. A denser source, like one of the closer-to-god peoples, has more of it to take. Only the morality changes. A Sentinel draining a prisoner into his armor and a Tender charging a blade from themselves are doing the same thing. Whose ember it is decides which of them is the monster.

Charge can't move from one vessel to another. Once ember is in a vessel, the only way out is to be spent doing what that vessel does, so you can't pour it off, stockpile it, or trade it. It always takes a living Kindled to fill a vessel, whoever's ember goes in. The exception is a **battery**, a vessel built to store ember and hand it back to other vessels. Batteries are rare specialist work, almost all of it owned by the Empire and the Church. A power that needs to move charge to places its people can't reach has a use for them. A lone Tender doesn't.

### Vigil-Blades

A vigil-blade is a Tender's sword, made of soulsteel. A Tender spends their apprenticeship working toward one and carries it for the rest of their life as proof of what they are.

The blade cuts with what's inside it. Anything it touches along the line of the edge, flesh or iron or stone, gets its ember pulled out so fast that the material gives way. It isn't sharp in the usual sense. It kills a thin line of whatever it hits and lets it fall apart. The wounds look wrong. Their edges are already gray and frostbitten, with no clean bleeding, the cut a hair-thin strip of desert opened in living meat. Frost forms in the air around it. In the dark a bare blade holds a faint gleam from whatever is bound inside it. A sensitive feels it before they see it, as pressure and cold and the sense of standing too close to someone grieving.

Earning one is the coming-of-age rite. A Shaper forges a new vessel or, far more often now, prepares an old one whose last master has been spent. Then the apprentice does the part only they can do, which is seeding it. They pour a measure of their own ember into the prepared steel to wake it up and bond with it. That's the real test. If you pour too little, the blade stays dead and you've failed. If you pour too much, the vessel, which was built to drink, takes more than you can afford and hollows you out or kills you. You have to know your own depth. Passing means you know yourself well enough to give the right amount of yourself to something made to take everything. From then on the blade is partly, literally, you. It's seeded with your ember, faintly stamped with who you are, and bonded to you without any taming.

At first, the blade runs on you. It needs a reservoir to be anything but dead steel. For a young Tender, the reservoir is the Tender. Every fight costs a little of you, paid back afterward in the slow work of refilling it, the first steps down toward a candle-end. The bond is keyed to your ember. A blade won't spend its own charge for a hand it doesn't know, so a stranger who swings it pays the cost out of themselves as the cold creeps up their arm. A stolen vigil-blade is about as likely to kill the thief as help them, which is why the Vigil's blades stay in the line.

Everything changes decades later, when a Tender's master dies. A master near the end can die into their student's blade, pouring all of their remaining ember into it as a last act. After that the blade runs on the master instead of the student, who doesn't have to burn themselves to use it anymore, because the master took on that cost. The blade is as full and steady as it will ever be. The master is in it too, since soul and fuel are the same stuff and stored ember always carries some of whoever it came from. Over the years the student uses the master up. The fuel thins and the voice fades, until the blade is empty and quiet and its wielder has to fill it from themselves again in old age. Then the student, a master now, dies into their own apprentice's blade. The empty one goes back to a Shaper to be prepared for someone new. Each generation's death pays for the next generation's best years.

> #### Wraith-blades
>
> You can fuel the same craft with a stolen death. When a Witherer quenches an unwilling death into the steel, what wakes up is a torn, terrified, furious thing that never agreed to any of this. It gives power but it fights the hand holding it. It also pushes, nudging the wielder toward killing, because the death inside is still trying to finish and wants to drag others down for company.
>
> A wraith-blade burns hot and fast. The fuel is straining to get out, so the blade gives sudden, brutal power and then runs down much faster than a vigil-blade holding the same amount of ember. The Turned have to keep feeding them killings to keep them full.
>
> Side by side they look the same. Only a Listener with a hand on each could tell a dying teacher's gift from a screaming murder. Over time, though, they age in opposite directions. An old vigil-blade turns up full and inhabited and ready to use. An old wraith-blade turns up burnt out, starving, and hostile.

### Resting-Stones

A **resting-stone** is the Vigil's other way of keeping its dead. When a Tender chooses to die into a vessel, they can pick a resting-stone instead of a blade, a soulsteel vessel built to hold a master and let them answer questions. A blade keeps the master with you to fight. A stone keeps the master to teach.

It's the closest thing the Vigil has to a library, except the book is a person. To wake the stone and talk to the master inside, you feed it ember. Every conversation uses up a little of them. The teacher doesn't last forever. A freshly bound, well-charged stone is almost the person themselves, present and talkative and able to teach in depth. A stone that's been consulted for years is wearing thin, its answers shorter and its presence fainter. A spent stone is cold metal with the last traces of someone in it, a teacher talked to death.

For a hunted tradition that passes everything by word of mouth, that's a brutal trade-off. The more precious the knowledge and the more it's needed, the faster you lose the person who has it. A cell has to decide whether to wake the old Shaper-stone to teach a new Shaper the rite, knowing it costs part of the only one who still remembers, or leave the stone quiet and risk the knowledge dying with the living.

### Other Vessels

The same craft can make anything that needs to hold ember. Every vessel is serious work for a Shaper, so all of them are rare and valuable.

A **crossing-core** is a big, crude vessel that leaks its charge out into the grays, so the deadzone drinks from the core instead of from the people around it. It's rough work, quick to charge and not picky about where its ember comes from. Caravans and graywalker crews huddle around one to survive a crossing. A core is a major asset, closely guarded and impossible to replace.

**Sentinel-armor** is soulsteel plate made into a full suit, a reservoir worn on the body that stores the ember a Sentinel runs on. It's finer work than a core and cruder than a blade. It's charged ahead of time and worn like any armor. Once it runs dry, it becomes the Sentinel's weak point.

A vessel can also be built to do a rough version of a discipline. A **bottled discipline** might be a force-vessel that can knock a door off its hinges, or one that burns. They eat a lot of ember and only get a few uses per charge, so people save them for the moment they're needed most. Only the blunt outward disciplines bottle well. Delicate living work like Mending or the Voice needs a person doing it in the moment, so it doesn't go into steel easily.

You don't need to be Kindled to use a vessel. An ordinary graywalker crew can run a crossing-core. Anybody can trigger a force-vessel, which is how a smuggling crew can get by without a mage. The rare talent is in making and charging soulsteel. That still doesn't add up to armies carrying vessel-weapons, because there aren't anywhere near enough Shapers. Whoever has the craft concentrates it. The Empire pours its small supply into a few terrifying Sentinels and a handful of precious cores instead of equipping its soldiers. An old vessel found in the dark is a masterwork worth more than gold.

> #### The willing dead, set to other purposes
>
> Most of the willing dead go into blades to fight or stones to teach, but the rite can do other things. A dead Tender might be bound into a wardstone to watch over a cell's meeting place, or set into a doorway as a guardian. Some of the Vigil's hidden places are guarded by their own dead.

## The Old Vigil

There was once a civilized age, with temples and masters and a Vigil that drew on the world freely, because the world seemed endless. They called the ember the Wellspring and built their greatness on drinking from it. That was the original sin. There's no such thing as a well that never runs dry.

Back then they called themselves the **Ember Vigil**. They meant it proudly, as keepers and stewards, masters of the power that lit the age. Their members were Tenders, people who tend the ember. The name took on another meaning later, once the sharpest of them figured out that touching the ember at all drains something that's dying. Proud stewardship turned into a deathbed watch over something they'd been helping to kill. Tending came to mean tending the dying and tending their bound dead.

Finding that out broke them apart. Some swore never to take from anything but themselves. They kept the craft but used it sparingly, spending their own lives to do it, and their numbers dwindled. Others decided that if the world was dying anyway they might as well drink. They grew strong and terrible, and the Turned descend from them. Neither side came out clean. One group is honest and dwindling. The other has no principles left and is very much still around.

Then the Emperor blamed them for everything. He wasn't wrong. They did spend the world, only on a far smaller scale than the accusation claims. They can't deny any of the charges, only the size of them. Nobody's willing to hear that while the man draining the world points at them as proof.

There's no order anymore, only the **Old Vigil**, a name that sounds like mourning, the way people talk about "the old country." It survives in the smallest form a craft can take and still last, one person who knows and one who's learning.

### How a Tender Lives

The tradition runs on pairs, a master and an apprentice.

For years the two travel together while the master teaches hand to hand, since things you have to feel can't be taught any other way. The apprentice trains on the master's own vigil-blade. Once the grandmaster has died into it, that means working with the grandmaster too, so the dead teacher and the living one teach side by side. If the grandmaster dies partway through the training, the apprentice feels them arrive in the steel. The blade goes back to the master after every lesson.

By the time the apprentice comes of age and seeds a blade of their own, the master has usually spent too much of themselves to stay on the road or keep up with a world that wants them dead, so the two split up. The master's last instruction is to go find an apprentice of their own, which turns the grief of parting into something that keeps going. The student becomes a link in the middle of the chain, somebody's former student and somebody's new teacher at once.

When the master is close to death, the cell gathers and the master dies into the apprentice's blade, with the apprentice's hand on the quench. In most lineages, the student is the one who finishes off their teacher and seals them in. It's an act of love and an act of killing at once. Whoever does it carries the mark. From then on the apprentice fights on their teacher's ember, with their teacher present in the steel. The master's own blade has gone quiet by then, the grandmaster long since spent, and it goes to the cell's Shaper to be prepared for some future apprentice.

### Cells

The Vigil can't gather in one place without getting caught, so it turned scattering into a rule. A **cell** forms around a Shaper, because everyone eventually needs one, since only a Shaper can forge a vessel or perform a binding. Members spread out for years at a time, each learning their own discipline, traveling into regions the others have never seen and tracking down half-remembered techniques. Then they come home.

A gathering is a reunion, a school, and a funeral rolled together. It's the only place a hunted Tender has people who know them. Everyone shares what they've brought back, argues about it, tests it, and carries it away again in their heads, so the cell works like a shared mind that syncs up a few times a decade. When a member dies, it loses a branch and keeps the tree. The bindings happen at gatherings too, so any gathering might be where an elder dies into a blade.

A gathering is also when a cell is most at risk. With everyone in one room, they can all be taken at once. The rules are strict. They never meet in the same place twice. The location gets passed along in ways that can't be tortured out of someone who doesn't know it yet. Everyone assumes any gathering might be betrayed.

What a cell fears most is losing its Shaper, because nobody can bind the binder. A Shaper can make blades but can't easily be made into one. If a cell's Shaper dies without a trained successor, the cell can't make vessels anymore. It can only inherit and hand down what already exists, slowly going sterile. Raising the next Shaper is the most anxious project a cell has. A Shaper who feels death coming without a successor might hang on past their time, drinking from the world or worse to buy a few more years. That's its own slow way of going bad.

Over time the branches drift apart. Knowledge lives in people's heads and in stones instead of in books, with nobody who has the authority to settle arguments. Two cells descended from the same Shaper long ago might practice nothing alike now. When separate lineages come together at a gathering, it's a clash of dialects. Sometimes everyone learns something. Other times it turns into a quiet split over whose version of a rite is the right one.

## The Turned

The Vigil calls the predators **the Turned**, the ones who turned the grip around and take instead of spending. They say it with grief, because every Turned started out Kindled like anyone else, maybe even as a Tender, before crossing a line. Another word, **the Faithless**, means the same people, named for breaking faith with the cost.

The Church has its own, more demonic words for them. To most people the Turned are the worst thing the Kindled can become. They're also real, which is handy for the Emperor. He hunts them toward extinction. They're monsters, but that isn't the main reason. Their power is the same kind as his, which makes them the only mages who might recognize what he is or look at a Sentinel and see a brother. He points at real monsters and tells the world this is what the Kindled are. Meanwhile the gentlest of the Vigil, the only people who know the world is being killed, get hunted right alongside them.

## Erdana's Light

**Erdana's Light** is the state faith. It worships Erdana as the goddess of life, a radiant presence who blesses the green and the living and watches over creation. None of the faithful know that the world itself is her body. The goddess and the world share a name because people once understood them as one. Long before the Empire, that understanding wore away, until "Erdana" meant two separate things, the goddess people pray to and the everyday word for the ground, the place, everything. People say it a hundred times a day with no idea they're standing on the god they worship.

The faith calls her light a holy radiance over creation. What they're worshiping is the visible glow of a god bleeding out.

### The Emperor's Blessing

The Church teaches that Wylter the Eternal doesn't die because he's just, that his rule is so righteous Erdana smiles on him and lets him keep going. His deathlessness counts as proof of his virtue. The longer he lives, the holier people think he is.

The thing that ought to give him away, a man who's outlived everyone and can't age or die, is the thing the faith points to as proof he's holy. Anyone who feels uneasy about it already has an answer waiting. Of course he doesn't die. Erdana wants it that way. Finding his immortality creepy means finding Erdana's blessing creepy, which is heresy. Doctrine catches the doubt and turns it into reverence before it's even fully formed.

In truth, Wylter steals his life from Erdana. She's dying under him. The blessed light the faithful take for heaven's favor is her own ember bleeding into the man sitting on her heart. "Erdana smiles on Wylter" is the core of the faith, taught to every child.

When someone does learn the truth and says it out loud, it sounds like every other heresy the world already expects from the Kindled.

## The Sentinels

The **Sentinels** are the Church's enforcers and the Vigil's hunters. "Sentinel" is the proud, holy title, the watchmen of the faith. People who know better, the bitter and the underworld, call them **the Wrought**, quietly, because they're made things, built out of stolen ember from childhood. A scared villager says a Sentinel rode into town. Somebody who understands says the Wrought are here.

The picture everyone has is a seven-foot man in heavy enchanted armor that covers every inch of him, bigger and more imposing than anything natural. They're grown that way, built up from childhood with ember that isn't theirs, made bigger and stronger and faster. The process also cracks them open, prying the channel wide until they can do what almost nobody on the ladder can and drink from the world itself. It's a slow trickle next to what a Sentinel burns, but it never stops. A natural world-drinker gets there at the end of a lifetime of discipline. A Sentinel is a kid forced open to it before he's grown. Whatever that does to a person, the helm hides it.

Under the holy steel, a Sentinel is a Quickener and a Witherer the Church raised on purpose. The faith that calls the Kindled **Eaters** and hunts them for spending the world makes its own enforcers by pouring other people's ember into children, so its holiest soldiers are built out of the same sin the Vigil is damned for. That's part of why the Turned get hunted so hard. A captured Turned is dangerous in a fight and more dangerous if he starts talking.

Sentinels burn ember far faster than the world can refill them, so the Church keeps them fed. The Kindling, the Church's own mages, pour their ember into Sentinels and call it a divine gift. They're the Vigil's renunciants flipped inside out, sensitives who spend themselves gladly, age wrong, hollow out, and die young, honored for every year they give away. When donated ember runs low, the Church tops the Sentinels up with the condemned, draining prisoners as their execution, so the killing and the refueling happen at once. A captured Tender gets spent into the thing that judged them. A Sentinel who's served long enough carries a graveyard of everyone he's sentenced.

The armor is a vessel itself, a reservoir he wears that stores the ember driving him. Out of his plate, a Sentinel looks too big and somehow hollow, more unsettling than he is sealed up. The suit can be charged ahead of time, which lets him fight at full strength in bursts without a mage at his side. It's also where he's vulnerable. Cut off from his charged plate, or once it runs dry, he's a big, strong, strange man and nothing more. To beat one, catch him cold, make him burn through the reservoir, then fight what's left.

His authority can't be appealed. It's supposed to be insight granted by Grace, so questioning a Sentinel's verdict means questioning the faith. One man can roam a continent handing out death with every act beyond question. That authority comes down from the Emperor.

### The Making

Sentinels are made out of stolen children over the course of a whole childhood. The training and the forging are one process with one goal, which is to shape an instrument.

A Sentinel starts as a Kindled child taken young, because the channel has to be opened before it sets and the body shaped while it's still growing. Some are handed over by frightened parents who believe the Church when it calls this an honor, a duty, or a mercy. Others are bought, or taken outright. Either way, the child is pulled out of the life they were born into and delivered to the forge before they're old enough to say no. That first theft, of the life the child never gets to have, is what everything else is built on.

Over the years that follow, ember-scars are cut into the child and deepened as they grow, to open the channel wider. It's deliberate, repeated wounding that shows for the rest of their life as a dull orange glow under the skin. The child is fed ember to grow past human size, the body turning superhuman over those same years. All along, the child is drilled, pushed in combat past what would break an ordinary kid, since an ember-fed body can take it. The Church cuts two grooves into every child, the Quickener's and the Witherer's. Quickening gets the most out of the body it's building. Withering lets a Sentinel close on a mage, drain them dry by hand, and burn the stolen ember himself. The two grips are nothing alike, and forcing both into a growing child is part of why some don't survive. Their fighting style is built around the strength and speed and toughness they're being given, so they learn to fight as the thing they're becoming. The training covers everything, armed and unarmed, one opponent or a crowd, because a Sentinel is supposed to be the Church's answer to whatever it runs into. Most of all it covers the Kindled and the horrors of the dying world, since hunting mages is the job. A Sentinel is built to close in on and kill the kinds of opponents who'd give a strong man trouble.

Sentinels are made in cohorts, a generation of stolen kids raised together. For most of them that's the only companionship they'll ever have. Some die along the way, since not every child survives the carving and feeding and drilling. The rest become the chapter they'll serve in, which is as close to family as any of them gets.

The mental shaping holds the rest of it together. It's also the part most likely to give. The Church raises a Sentinel in total certainty about divine authority and his own righteousness, because a tool that doubts is useless. A child who's never known anything else, with no other life to compare it to, told from the beginning that the pain and the carving are making him into something holy, believes it completely. That certainty becomes the frame his whole self is built on, put up before he could fight it. When a Sentinel does come loose, because his chapter falls or his faith finally cracks, the entire structure of meaning the Church built into him comes down with it. The betrayal goes as deep as the conditioning did.

The making takes his childhood, his body, and his mind, and leaves him no life outside of it. His reward is to be a weapon, aimed wherever the Church wants and used up in its service, valued as a tool and never as a person. He carries it in his body, his scars, and his certainty for the rest of his life.

### Hunter Teams

A seven-foot man in sealed armor can't investigate anything, question a scared village, or read a room, so a Sentinel never travels alone. A team is a single Sentinel and his support, usually a mage and one other.

The Sentinel is the judgment and the muscle. He's the icon, the one who makes the call and does the killing.

His first support is usually a mage, though which discipline depends on the team and the job. A Mender is the usual pick, since a body worked as hard as a Sentinel's needs looking after. A Voice is just as handy for getting people talking and calming a hostile town, while a Listener can sense things hiding where the giant can't. Whatever their discipline, the mage usually ends up as the team's face. They do the talking and the questioning and the reading of a room that goes quiet the second a Sentinel ducks through the door. In a pinch they can feed him from their own ember like a walking Kindling. In that job the same pair of hands does saint's work and monster's work, healing or charming a village in the morning and helping drain its hidden Tender that night.

The other is usually a servant, a scout and forager and legman who can pass for nobody and slip into taverns and markets where a holy giant and a robed mage would shut everybody up. Servants are trained hard and trusted with dangerous work. They can handle themselves in a fight and are often the ones who find the target in the first place. Of the team, the servant is often the most dangerous, because nobody thinks to be afraid of them until it's too late.

> #### Using hunter teams
>
> Every team has its own history, friction, and loyalties. If you want to complicate the hunters or make them human, leave the Sentinel's icon alone and work through the mage or the servant, the people who see what he is and stay anyway, or who slowly find they can't.

## The Geography of a Dying World

### The Deadzones

A **deadzone** is land Erdana has pulled out of. Nobody poisoned or cursed it. The ember that used to be in the soil and air and everything living there has drained away or been drawn toward the center, leaving matter without the stuff the world runs on. The faithful would never put it this way, but the deadzones are dead patches on a living body, gangrene spreading across a god.

Graywalkers and Tide-readers call them **graylands**, or the grays, because that's how they look to someone who can see gleam, flat, unlit gray where the light should be. People who've started to understand what Erdana is sometimes call them **scars**.

A living thing is full of ember and a deadzone is empty. The world can't stand that difference. Walk into one and it pulls the ember out of you the entire way, slow and steady. Long before thirst or heat could get you, the ground has drunk you down to a husk. The narrow ones are survivable if you're fast and strong and have enough to lose. The wide ones can't be crossed by normal means at all. That dead land is what has kept the living regions apart.

Rot is a kind of life too. With none left to do the work, nothing in a deadzone decays. The place is full of things kept as they fell. Husk-trees stand gray and whole. Animals and people lie in the positions they died in, mummified by the emptiness. Cross a deep one and you walk past the perfectly preserved dead of everyone who tried before you, each frozen in the moment they failed, which tells you pretty clearly what's coming. There are no insects and no birds, nothing alive to make a sound. For a sensitive it's worse than silence. It's a hole in the world. Their sense goes blind and cold, with a kind of vertigo from feeling their own ember wick away the whole time they walk.

### Crossing

Getting across alive is a craft. The people who can do it are some of the most valuable and least respectable people around.

The idea is to feed the land so it doesn't feed on you. A crossing-core bleeds its charge into the emptiness and pays the toll so the travelers don't have to. The party huddles around it in the dark, watching its glow fade as it spends, racing the dimming light against the distance left, with smaller personal vessels held back in case something goes wrong.

A Tide-reader leads the way, because the graylands aren't uniform. The ember pulled out unevenly, leaving threads where a little life hangs on and dead hollows that can drain a person dry in minutes. Only a Tide-reader can find the safe path. A route through a deadzone is hard-won knowledge, guarded jealously and killed over. It also goes stale, since the grays keep spreading and a good route can be dead in ten years. A graywalker's routes are worth more than their vessels.

The deepest crossings might only be survivable by feeding the land someone else's ember. A desperate or predatory guide drains his own clients, or brings along the condemned and the trafficked as fuel, spending their lives into the grays to get the paying passengers across.

> #### Graywalkers and the truth
>
> The Tide-reader is the discipline that could expose the Emperor. Most Tide-readers end up guiding crossings, a job that needs their gift and sends them all over the world reading the flow wherever they go. The underworld is full of the kind of mind most likely to stumble onto the secret, all of them working as navigators. The person who finally figures out what's under Abira could easily be some old graywalker who spent forty years reading the waste and slowly noticed it all drains toward one place.

### The Shape of the World

The withdrawal has a direction. Erdana pulls her ember in toward the Heart and lets go of the edges, so the land is most alive near the center and dies from the outside in. That pattern sits on top of a real landmass with mountains, rivers, basins, and coasts. The grays follow the terrain. Waste collects in flat open basins where nothing slows the draining. Rough highlands stay green long after the lowlands around them have died. A living river can run like a green thread far out into dying country. The result is a patchwork of living pockets and dead stretches, broken up unevenly, with the richest green near Abira and the deepest grays out where Erdana let go first.

The held core is Abira and the deep green country around it, where the throne's power is total and taken for granted. The law gets enforced, the Church is everywhere, and Sentinels are stationed there. Not much waste gets in the way of travel, which is a big part of why control is so complete. People raised there have never seen the Empire as anything but real and fair. Loyalty there is the most sincere in the Empire, because nothing has ever tested it.

The throttled middle is where the green starts to fray and smaller grays start to wind between regions. Authority shows up in waves, tax collectors and garrisons and the occasional Sentinel passing through. In between, the regions more or less run themselves. It's the worst setup to live under. People get the full weight of the oppression without the thick imperial fabric that makes obedience in the core feel natural. They're worn down. They comply perfectly and feel none of it. The gap between the loyalty they show and the loyalty they feel is widest here, which makes the middle the best ground for the resistance.

The edge is the far country, cut off from the rest of the world by the great grays, where the Empire is a claim on paper that nobody enforces. Some places out there are lawless. Authority evaporated and nobody replaced it, so frontier towns are run by whoever's strong or trusted, under a banner nobody bothers to take down. Some have become resistance ground, where the Empire's absence let something organized grow, with strongholds where the Old Vigil lives more openly and oppressed communities govern themselves, too far away for the throne to bother crushing until it decides they're worth the cost. Others are fiefdoms, where a local lord has swapped imperial law for his own while still flying the Empire's colors, rules however he likes, and calls it the Emperor's will. A fiefdom often looks more loyal than the core, since nobody's close enough to check. The Emperor has never heard of half these lords and would disown them if he had.

The Empire only reaches as far as its hands can, whatever the maps say. The edge meets the real Empire only when a Sentinel team shows up, a judgment crossing the grays into some place that had half forgotten the throne was real. For as long as they stay, the Empire is real, brutal, and absolute. Then they leave and it goes back to being a name.

The Empire can cross the grays. The Church outfits sanctioned caravans with crossing-cores charged by the Kindling and with condemned prisoners drained into the grays to fuel the trip. A big imperial caravan is the whole Empire in miniature, with holy mages spending themselves at the front out of devotion, the condemned spent at the back as executions, and the Sentinels riding untouched through the middle while everyone around them pays the toll. It costs ember paid out in lives, the scarcest currency there is, so the throne can't do it often. That cost is why the edge sees the Empire so rarely. Smugglers cross cheap and quiet and often. They're weaker than the throne but reach the edge far more easily. Out in the far green, their shadow networks rival the Empire's own.

### The Sea

The land is dying and the sea isn't, because the sea isn't Erdana. The ocean is something older and different, alive on its own terms in a way that has nothing to do with ember, so it doesn't drain like the grays or glow like the green. To a Tide-reader, the gleam of the land runs right up to the waterline and stops. Past that, the sea is a huge blank their sense slides off, alive but unreadable. The Kindled are weaker at sea, cut off from what they live in. They can spend the ember they're carrying, but there's no living world around them to draw from.

The sea is no escape from the dying. Its life can't replace what Erdana's losing. Nobody can live on the water, either, so the end still comes for everything on land. What the sea does offer is connection, where the grays only cut things off. It links the coasts it touches, carries ships, and softens the deadening along the shore, so coastal country stays greener and better connected than the failing interior. A ship can sail around a dead expanse that would kill anyone trying to walk it. The shipping network runs alongside the gray routes and competes with them. Port cities are looser, more open, and harder to watch than inland towns. The sea is the only direction where the world isn't closing in. Whatever's out in its deepest waters is a mystery of a completely different kind from anything on the dying land.

### Abira, the Heart

**Abira** sits in the most alive country in the world. The green gets thicker for days of travel in every direction around the city. All of the world's ember runs toward the city on its way to the Heart, so the land around it stays soaked even while Erdana's core beneath it is hollowed out. Farmland gives way to deeper, older growth. Rivers run full even in the worst summers. The soil gets so black that pilgrims take it as a sign they're close to holy ground. The old gardens inside the walls have to be cut back constantly or they'd swallow the streets.

The city is built in rings. At the center, on the holiest ground, is the **Hallowseat**, the Emperor's citadel. It's the most heavily defended place in the world and the one that least needs it, since the faith of everyone around it protects it better than any wall.

Around it is the sacred core, with the great temples, the seminaries, and the homes of high clergy and senior officials. The closer you get to the center, the harder it is to get in, until the citadel itself is closed to everyone.

The pilgrim districts surround the sacred core and live off the business of arrival, lodging houses, offering-sellers, guides, and places to eat. They're crowded, prosperous, and a bit predatory. Strange faces are normal there, so a stranger can disappear into the crowd.

The high districts hold the old power, the great families settled in the deepest, oldest green. Loyalty politics gets played for the highest stakes here, where being close to the Hallowseat is the only currency that counts.

The working city is the big, busy bulk of Abira, crafts and markets and river quarters and ordinary neighborhoods, well off by the world's standards and closely watched.

The enclaves sit toward the outer city on worse ground, where the diaspora peoples, the poor, and the marginal end up. They're oppressed and watched from outside but close-knit inside. The informer network has a weaker grip there than anywhere else in the city, which makes them natural places for contraband, rumors, and people on the run.

The outer edge and the gates are where the pilgrim roads come in and the traffic of the whole world funnels through, transient and mixed and full of holes, where people and goods enter the city before they get sorted inward.

Abira is magnificent, the richest, most beautiful, and holiest city in the world. It works, too. The green is real and the faith is sincere.

## The Peoples of Erdana

The Empire's faith puts humans on top. Humans are Erdana's rightful children, while the other peoples are lesser, impure, or malformed. Their suffering out on the dying rim gets treated as proof of their lower status, instead of as the same death that's coming for everybody. The non-human peoples end up together, partly out of solidarity and partly because they're shut out, in enclaves, quarters, and undercities, oppressed and watched. Those enclaves become natural homes for the Old Vigil, the smugglers, and anyone else with a reason to hate the throne.

Because the land dies from the edges in, peoples have been moving inward for centuries, fleeing the rim and drifting, without knowing it, toward the thing that's eating their homelands.

#### Diaspora

Their homelands on the rim are already dead. They fled inward generations ago and live in the heartland as displaced enclaves, carrying a country that doesn't exist anymore. Their young have never seen the green their elders grieve for.

#### Frontier

Their homelands in the outer green are still alive but dying, cut off from the heartland by the dead seas. They're proud, distinct, still whole, and living on borrowed time, where the diaspora peoples were a century ago. You can visit them in their own land and see it's already slipping away.

#### Margin

They've adapted to low-ember conditions and live where the green frays and others can't, which makes them essential as graywalkers and feared for it. Being despised and indispensable at the same time is how you get a powerful, resentful underworld. The physics defines them more than where they live.

#### Closer-to-god

They're made of denser or stranger ember, closer to the source. The Church calls them holy or abominable. Either label is deadly, since holy means being used as sacred material and abominable means being hunted. Denser ember is more ember, a richer well to drink from, which puts them close to the central secret. The Sentinels prize them for draining. The Emperor himself might have an interest in them.

## The Resistance

The Empire creates its own opposition just by how it runs. Its culture is cut-throat by design. Everyone watches everyone else, turning in a neighbor proves your own loyalty, and anyone could be working against the Light. A place like that turns out performers more than believers. Most people put on a show of loyalty for each other all day long because that's how you survive. The resistance lives in the gap between the loyalty everyone shows and the loyalty almost nobody feels. That gap is widest in the worn-down middle.

The opposition that exists stays splintered because it has to. A cell that doesn't know about the others can't betray them, can't be tortured into giving them up, and can't take them down with it when it falls. The network is built on purposeful ignorance, each knot knowing only its own members and maybe a contact or two. The price is that nothing can coordinate, grow big, or turn into a movement with one will. What it can do is pass small things down the line, a warning, a caravan's route, a name to stay away from, a trick that worked. When an imperial caravan passes through, someone notes it and sends word ahead so another group can take advantage. Smugglers carry these whispers along the same routes through the grays that they use for contraband.

Tenders are the network's patrons and the thread that ties it together. They're hunted, so they lean toward the resistance against the shared enemy. They're always moving, because a Tender who stays put gets caught. That forced wandering is the kind of movement a splintered network needs. They also carry knowledge and a long view. A Tender, or a master and apprentice, can take on several groups and circulate among them, turning up to teach and steady them and carry word from the last group to the next without naming anyone, then moving on before they draw a team down on anybody. A Tender can't stick around long enough to lead. They're a mentor, a courier, and a living library to a handful of groups who never learn about each other, only about the Tender who comes and goes. If the Tender falls, the groups they looked after are orphaned but not exposed, because the connections only lived in the Tender's head.

The resistance doesn't know what winning would mean, because none of them have worked out the truth. Their instincts tell them, correctly, that the Empire is rotten and hypocritical and grinding the world and its people down. Their plan goes about as far as killing the Emperor, followed by a vague "after" where whoever's left finds some way to heal the world. They believe taking the Empire apart will make room for a fix. Nothing they can see would warn them otherwise.

No group, however big, can survive a team of Sentinels kicking down the door. Anything large enough to matter is large enough to be found and wiped out. Once a target finally justifies the crossing, the Empire only needs to spare one Sentinel and his team. The same caution that keeps the cells ignorant of each other keeps them small, while the Empire prunes them from outside. Seeds grow, get noticed, and get wiped out. The network sends news of each loss down the line as a warning. The resistance can't win. It can keep going, teach, pass word along, and keep the idea alive in small hidden pockets.

## The Bound Dead

In this world, sticking around after death is normal. The ember never completely leaves. That's why there's anything to draw on at all and why the Vigil can bind its dead into steel.

#### The lingering dead

These are everywhere and harmless. An ordinary death leaves a residue that fades as the ember scatters, so the freshly dead are faintly present and the long dead are gone. Hauntings are mundane, part of everyday life. A Listener feels them and ordinary people pick them up as a mood. Places where a lot of people died hold residue. Battlefields are thick with it.

#### The stuck dead

These are rare and wrong. A death that's sudden enough, unwilling enough, or soaked in enough feeling can bind itself to a place or object with no craft involved. They're always the bad deaths, because a peaceful death scatters and a violent one clings, holding onto only the screaming part, instinct and hunger and rage. Laying one to rest means letting the bound ember finally scatter, which is mercy and exorcism at once.

#### The made dead

These are rare and terrible. To make a corpse walk, you bind ember into it like a vessel and fuel it, so the walking dead run on the same ladder as everything else. They're an atrocity of waste before they're an atrocity of violation. A hollow construct is a mindless puppet powered by raw ember. A bound construct, driven by one particular torn death, is a wraith-blade that walks, stupid and furious and hard to stop. The craft survives in a nearly lost line that almost nobody touches. One walking corpse can empty villages and bring Sentinels running.

#### The willing dead

These are the Vigil's sacrament, masters who choose at death to be bound into a vigil-blade or a resting-stone. The Vigil carries its dead in these two kinds of vessels and rations them, fighting on its ancestors and learning from them while losing a little of them with every fight and every lesson.

> #### The Emperor among the dead
>
> The Emperor is a willing construct on an enormous scale, a self kept going past its death by ember that isn't his. The Church condemns binding the dead and hunts vigil-blades and resting-stones as witchcraft, all while serving a man who is a death that won't scatter, running on an artery into a dying god. Every kind of bound dead is a small version of him. He's the only one that's ever fully worked. With nothing like him to compare him to, nobody recognizes what he is.

## The Horrors

Erdana is a fantasy world, so the usual bestiary is mostly there, beasts and old monsters and all the normal dangers of wild places. The dying adds a twisted layer on top of that, one that comes from inside the world. There's no demon realm and no invading evil. Everything monstrous here grows out of the dying, the ember economy, and the bound dead. New ones keep turning up, because Erdana is still making them as she dies.

#### Wrong-beasts

Wildlife goes wrong at the edges of the grays. Anything living in long-term ember starvation is under heavy pressure to get ember back, so the edges of the deadzones are full of fast, ugly adaptation, with ordinary animals learning to drain as well as eat. There are pack hunters that leave their kills gray, whole, and untouched, because the meat was never what they were after. Some grazers hold their ember so tightly that anything biting one gets drained in return. This is the everyday danger on the frontier, ordinary in feel, the wildlife of the thinning lands learning to take the way the grays do.

#### Kindled beasts

Kindled beasts are a wilder version of the same pressure. Animals are born Kindled too. A Kindled animal has the ability with no mind to hold it back. The classic case is a creature that Quickens on instinct, burning ember for strength and speed with no sense of the cost, so it keeps drawing and keeps growing into a huge, half-starved monster driven mad by its own hunger, needing more and more to keep up what it's built. Most never tip into that spiral, but when one does it's a local disaster. There's no malice in it, only an appetite it can't escape.

#### Husks

The grays can move the dead themselves. Most of the husks preserved in the waste lie still forever, but a husk that's been lying in a hungry stretch long enough can get moved by the deadzone's own pull. The land reaches for the only warm living thing for miles through whatever's closest and drags dead matter upright to close the distance. Husks are the deadzone's antibodies, neither alive nor bound. Graywalkers fear a stretch thick with husks more than plain empty ground, because the empty ground at least stays where it is.

#### The deliberate dead

The deliberate dead are the work of the Turned. They're rare. A corpse run on raw ember is a mindless puppet and an atrocity of waste, the world's shrinking life spent so dead meat can carry and dig and kill. A corpse driven by a particular torn death sealed back into it is worse. It's fast and furious and stupid, hard to stop because it's already dead and running on borrowed ember. It only ends when the binding is released. When the drained victims of a Turned predator start getting up on their own, the hero is whoever tracks down the maker, since the walking dead are a symptom.

#### Erdana's dreams and fevers

Erdana makes things too. She's in pain and she dreams badly. Where the world is thinnest, a dream can catch enough ember to half exist. It's a nightmare with no biology and no sense to it, grief with just enough substance to touch you. It works on the mind and the ember more than the body, filling a place with dread and draining its warmth. A Tender who runs into one feels the sorrow in it and understands that they're looking at their god dreaming. There's no fighting a dream.

She also makes things on purpose, blindly, the way a body runs a fever. Monstrous things drawn to ember-use rise up to kill whatever's hurting her. They hunt sensitives, because the Kindled are what drain her. They can't tell a Tender keeping faith from a Turned gorging or a Sentinel killing, so her immune system goes after the people who might help her. They never form over Abira, where the ground is lush but her core beneath it is too drained to raise one, so she runs fevers against her friends at the edges and lies quiet over the wound at her center. A Tender hunted by one of these is being hunted by the thing they've given their life to protect, with no way to tell it they're on its side.

> #### Using the horrors
>
> An everyday local hero handles the wrong-beasts of the thinning land, which are common and dangerous. A hero with a reputation handles husks and risen bad deaths, which take real knowledge or luck. A regional hero hunts the Turned behind the deliberate dead. Erdana's own creations sit above all of that, less a fight than an encounter, the stuff legends and madmen are made of. They work best used sparingly. Few of these horrors hunt the way normal predators do. Most are drawn to ember because they're empty and you're full, the same logic as the grays, given a body.

## Wylter the Eternal

**Wylter** was a man's name once, before the titles. He wanted to live forever in a world with an end date. Instead of raging at the dark, he reverse-engineered a whole civilization to pull it off quietly.

Erdana had been worshiped as the goddess of life for longer than anyone could trace, long enough that her people had forgotten the world was her body. Wylter worked it out. He was born a generation or two after the Vigil broke apart, when the Kindled were already distrusted, and he built on both. The religion came first, because he couldn't tap the heart without controlling the ground over it and the story about what that ground was. As a young man he founded Erdana's Light, a new church around the old goddess. Over a long natural lifetime he made it the state faith, then built an empire on the excuse of hunting down the Kindled who'd spent the world. The persecution gave him a security perimeter and an alibi at the same time. As the world's leading expert on the danger of the Kindled, he became the last person anyone would suspect of running the biggest ember operation in history. He was an old man by the time the Hallowseat stood over the Heart. That was when he opened the artery, two hundred years ago.

Every public institution is a disguise for the machinery of one man's immortality. He built it out of true things, a sincere faith, a reasonable fear, and a real crime the Vigil committed. He didn't make any of it up. He arranged real facts to point the wrong way, which is why the lie holds.

Everyone else on the ladder drinks the runoff, the ember spread through the world. Wylter found a way to open the artery and drink from Erdana directly, at her heart. No other Kindled has come close. That's where his deathlessness and his unmatched power come from. The centuries-long timeline everyone accepts as the natural order is a measure of what he's stolen. He's been turning millennia into centuries and spending the world's future on himself.

All of this has made him the valve. He opened the wound with rites nobody else knows, and those rites are the only way to close it. If he dies before anyone gets them out of him, the wound tears wide open. Ember floods back into the world in huge waves. Deserts bloom, dead borders turn green, and the world drinks deep and celebrates. In about ten years it empties itself out completely and everything dies at once.

He can't be killed in any way that helps. Winning means taking him alive and getting the rites out of him before removing him. Any faction that only wants him dead is an apocalypse cult without realizing it, however heroic its members are.

Two hundred years in, the immortality has long since stopped feeling like a triumph and become his condition. Everyone he conquered, every architect of his religion, and every enemy who understood him died of old age generations ago. He's the last person alive who knows what the world is. He carries it the way an old man carries the last memory of a street that's been torn down. Two centuries early, he's already living as what he's working toward, the last survivor on a world that's forgotten it's dying. In his own mind, he's the only one with the nerve to refuse to die along with everyone else.

He rules from the Hallowseat in Abira, the Heart of Erdana, which anyone not standing on ceremony calls the Heart. The faithful kneel toward it as the seat of Erdana's favor.

## The Blackwater River

*A worked example of a frontier region, the kind of place the edge produces.*

The **Blackwater** is a cold river choked with fog, running from dead country down to the living sea. It's one of the last good routes through a dying frontier. There isn't much law or mercy along it. Money and traffic stick to the water because the water is the way through. So do the predators that money attracts.

It starts up in gray-locked deadzone country, where the cold of the emptied land hits the water and fog rises off it, then runs down through fraying country into a living lower river, a strip of green prosperity along the banks that thins out fast as you move away from them. Danger follows the river the way temperature does. It's deadly at the dead head, contested in the middle reaches where the raiding happens, and safe at the living mouth. In the dangerous stretches the river splits into foggy tributaries and shadowy side-channels, a maze where reavers wait in ambush, smaller predators work the edges, and whatever the dying world makes comes down out of the gray. A crew that knows the tributaries owns the river. Everyone else takes their chances.

**Blackthorn Hold** sits on a cliff at the dead head, locked in by the grays and reachable in practice only by water Varak controls. It's his lair and the place people vanish to, where captives get hauled up out of the living world into the dead one and nobody ever comes to rescue them. A ways downstream, Grayend is the last town before the dead country and the grimmest of the market towns, deepest in his shadow and existing only because he allows it, the first place his cargo hits the market. Past it there's nothing but fog and the Hold. Sand Run is a rough upriver market town in the contested reaches, full of fenced goods and holding pens. Riverport is a working trade town with a little power of its own, handling legitimate traffic and quietly handling Varak's too. Wavecrest, closer to the coast, is the nicest of the middle towns, richer and a little more ambitious.

**Threshwater** is the big city at the river mouth, the largest and richest on the river. It was built as a refuge for the peoples the Empire throws away and got rich on the steady stream of them arriving by sea and by caravan road. It's outward-facing, polyglot, and strong. Threshwater knows what Varak is and has an unspoken truce with him. The city's too well defended to raid, but it can't be sure it could stop an armored Sentinel determined to smash his way in. Neither side wants to find out. It's the safe end of the river, opposite the Hold's cruel one. Anyone running from him runs there.

Varak's business is a closed loop. He preys on the rich traffic of the contested middle reaches, the gilded pleasure barges, trade galleys, and noble convoys that have to run the gauntlet between the lower cities and the upper country. He leaves the small market towns alone because they're his markets, where his stolen goods get fenced and his collared captives get sold. He robs the river's rich and sells back into the same river, with the slaves and goods flowing into the towns the victims came from. That keeps the whole operation steady and hard to break up.

Threshwater is also one of the best footholds the resistance has on the edge, for the same reasons it became a refuge. It's free of the throne and wealthy, with sea trade and none of the informer culture that chokes resistance in imperial country. Its streets are full of people the Empire threw away, who have their own bitter take on what the Empire is. Here the network can grow from a thin thread into something like a community, a hub that gathers people, shelter, money, and knowledge while still keeping command spread out. Cells in the interior can lean on it as a refuge, a storehouse, and a switchboard without giving away their secrets. The Vigil's wandering Tenders use it as a waystation where they can rest and run into each other. That concentration is the resistance's biggest strength on the river and its biggest weakness, since a hub is the kind of gathering the resistance otherwise never risks.

Varak doesn't stand in the way of any of it. He wants the Empire torn down out of personal hatred, so the enemy of his enemy is welcome on his river. It's an ugly overlap of grudges that everyone involved knows better than to call an alliance.

## Varak Thorne, the Leash Lord

**Varak Thorne** is a renegade Sentinel who went his own way after the chapter he was stationed with was destroyed. He rules the Blackwater from Blackthorn Hold and commands the black war galley **The Black Harvest**, leading a disciplined crew of reavers who raid wherever money and beauty travel together. Valuables get stripped first. Then the survivors get sorted. The most attractive are collared in iron for training, use, and sale. The rest are used for the crew's amusement until their short lives run out, then disposed of casually. The Hold is where captives are broken, trained, stored, and sold.

The Church took him as a Kindled child, carved the ember-scars into him to pry his channel open, fed him ember to grow him past human size, and drilled him from childhood into a weapon, with nothing in his youth except the forging. He keeps quiet about how much of that is still in him and lets the frontier see a former Sentinel gone freelance, something people can understand and be afraid of.

The scars never go fully dark. They hold a dull orange glow like banked coals and flare up bright with emotion or effort, so the only way to hide them is to cover them. When he isn't channeling he can tamp them down to that low, steady glow. If the orange lines flare hot under his skin in a fight, he's stopped holding back. The scars are also his wound, a record of what was cut into him to make him useful. His power and his violation are the same marks. He can't use what he is without being reminded of how he was made.

His men know what he is in broad strokes. They know the Leash Lord used to be a Sentinel. They've seen the scars and felt the dread that comes off him, but they don't know how a Sentinel works, the ember, the draining, the armored war-engine. He has no plans to teach them.

Out of armor, which is how he lives and raids, he's an overbuilt fighting machine, superhumanly strong and fast and tough, with more combat skill than any veteran could pick up in a lifetime. No ordinary force can stand up to him. His technique is built on abilities normal fighters don't have, so fighting past human limits is how he works. Since he was trained specifically to kill the Kindled and the horrors, he's well equipped to deal with the gray-things and any rival mage on the river. He can briefly flare into something more by surging on his own ember, drinking from the world, or draining someone he's touching, but he keeps that hidden and rare. Without armor, he's limited to his own reserves and a slow draw from the world, which means he can't keep the magic going for long.

In armor, which is how he'd go to war, he's the full terror the Church built him to be. The soulsteel plate is a deep, charged reservoir that lets him run hot nonstop, nearly untouchable, a one-man catastrophe. With no Church to top him up, he's been hoarding charge in the suit for a long time, so when he finally puts it on, he may be carrying more than any working Sentinel ever does. Putting on the plate unlocks power he's banked in secret. He keeps it stored and charged at the Hold for when he needs it. The only limits are how deep the reservoir goes and the wear on aging plate that no Shaper looks after.

He fuels himself off the captives his trade throws away, draining the used-up ones before they're disposed of and hiding the ember theft inside a slaving business that was going to kill those people anyway.

He's a cynic. He doesn't know the deep truth about the world, which is a mercy, because if he did he'd be worse. A lifetime of fighting for someone else, rewarded with a pat on the head, gave him enough perspective, once he had some distance and freedom, to see the lies he was raised on, the horrors done to the children, and what was taken from him. The Church trained him into total certainty. When the chapter fell, that structure broke and let years of buried wrongness out all at once. His cynicism goes as deep as the conditioning did. He owes nobody anything now and takes what he wants, because everything was taken from him.

Before it all broke, he spent years as a true believer, riding with his team, hunting the Kindled, executing by draining, and doing the Church's horrors gladly because he was sure they were holy. He used to hunt the people he now grudgingly respects on his river. That blood is on him. Whatever destroyed his chapter freed him and bereaved him at the same time, taking the only people who'd ever shared his life. What it was is still an open question, a loaded thread that might still be reaching toward him.

Out on the frontier, where nothing can match a Sentinel, he's untouchable and his rule is absolute. An escaped Sentinel is an abomination and an embarrassment, though. The Church would wipe him out with special enthusiasm if it knew where he was and could spare the cost of crossing. He stays remote, locked in by the grays, hidden as just another reaver lord, free only because he isn't worth the Empire's trouble yet. The day he is, the only thing that can threaten him will cross the gray to find him, another Sentinel or a team of them, the scale of the life he ran from.

## Glossary

**Abira.** The capital and holiest city in the world, built in rings over the Heart. Called the Heart by anyone not standing on ceremony.

**Battery.** A vessel built to store ember and give it back to other vessels, the only exception to the rule that charge never moves between vessels. Rare specialist work, nearly all of it held by the Empire and the Church.

**Black Harvest, The.** Varak Thorne's black war galley, his raiding ship and transport on the Blackwater.

**Blackthorn Hold.** Varak Thorne's cliffside fortress at the gray-locked head of the Blackwater, where captives are broken, stored, and sold, and where his armor waits fueled.

**Blackwater, the.** A cold, fog-choked frontier river running from deadzone country to the living sea, ruled along its length by Varak Thorne.

**Closer-to-god peoples.** Peoples made of denser ember, nearer the source, and a richer well to drain. The Church names them holy or abominable.

**Crossing-core.** A large crude vessel that bleeds its charge into the grays so the deadzone drinks the core instead of the travelers.

**Deadzone.** Land Erdana has withdrawn from, emptied of ember, that drains the life out of anything living that enters. Also called the grays or graylands, and by some, scars.

**Drinking from the world.** The rarest way to fuel ember-use, pulling straight from the open, dying land. The top rung of the fuel ladder.

**Eaters.** The Church's slur for the Kindled, blaming them for spending the world.

**Ember.** The life of the world, Erdana's essence, sealed inside every living thing and thinning in the open world as she dies. Called Grace by the Church and the Wellspring by the old Vigil.

**Erdana.** The goddess of life the faithful worship, and the everyday word for the world. Almost no one knows the world is her body. A dying mind whose pain leaks as dread.

**Erdana's Light.** The state faith, founded by Wylter around the old worship of Erdana as the goddess of life. It doesn't know the world is her dying body, and it teaches that the Emperor's deathless reign is her blessing on his justice.

**Faithless, the.** A second Vigil word for the Turned, naming them for breaking faith with the cost.

**Gleam.** The light ember casts, faint to a Tide-reader, plain to any Kindled when strong, and visible to anyone from a vessel channeling at full. Shows how much charge a vessel holds.

**Grace.** The Church's word for ember.

**Grays, the.** The deadzones, named for how they look to a sight that reads gleam. Also graylands.

**Graywalker.** A guide who can cross the grays, reading safe paths and carrying a crossing-core. Graywalkers also carry the resistance's whispers.

**Hallowseat.** The Emperor's citadel at the center of Abira, over the Heart.

**Hand, the.** The discipline of moving things by gripping the ember-thread within them.

**Heart, the.** Abira, and the core of Erdana beneath it where the Emperor drinks.

**Kindled.** Anyone born able to feel and move ember, with no training implied.

**Kindling.** Kindled taken by the Church and spent as fuel, pouring their ember into the Sentinels and the crossings as a divine gift.

**Leash Lord, the.** Varak Thorne's byname along the Blackwater.

**Listener, the.** The discipline of opening to the ember and receiving, which means hearing Erdana herself. Mistaken for prophecy.

**Mender, the.** The discipline of healing by pouring ember into a wound. Shares a channel with the Witherer.

**Old Vigil, the.** What remains of the Ember Vigil, scattered into hidden master-and-apprentice cells and hunted.

**Quickener, the.** The discipline of burning ember into one's own flesh for impossible speed and strength.

**Resting-stone.** A soulsteel vessel holding a master who chose at death to be bound to teach, consulted by feeding it ember and depleted a little with each use.

**Sentinel.** The Church's armored giant and the Vigil's hunter, a child grown and opened on stolen ember into a seven-foot killer. Trained as both a Quickener and a Witherer. Called the Wrought by those who know what they are. Travels with a small support team, usually a mage and a servant.

**Shaper, the.** The discipline of acting on ember itself to transform matter, and the only one that can make soulsteel. The most guarded member of any Vigil cell.

**Soulsteel.** Steel a Shaper has worked to hold ember, the basis of every vessel. Called witch-steel by the Church.

**Tender.** A trained member of the Vigil, who tends the ember, the dying, and their bound dead.

**Threshwater.** The refuge-city at the mouth of the Blackwater, the richest on the river, built by the peoples the Empire discards. Holds an unspoken truce with Varak Thorne and a strong resistance foothold.

**Tide-reader, the.** The discipline of feeling ember move across the world, used to find living land and read routes through the grays.

**Turned, the.** The Vigil's word, said with grief, for those who take ember from others instead of spending their own. Predators, and the makers of wraith-blades.

**Varak Thorne.** The Leash Lord, a renegade Sentinel who rules the Blackwater as a reaver and slaver, hides the extent of his power, fuels himself off discarded captives, and hates the Empire that made him.

**Vessel.** Anything made of soulsteel, named for its purpose. Holds a charge of ember that shows as gleam, never leaks, and is spent only in use.

**Vigil-blade.** A Tender's soulsteel sword, earned by seeding it with one's own ember. It drains what it cuts and holds the master who dies into it.

**Voice, the.** The discipline of working the ember sealed inside another living person, by suggestion, compulsion, or taking.

**Wellspring.** The old Vigil scholars' word for ember, naming it as an endless source.

**Witherer, the.** The discipline of drawing ember out of a living thing by hand, which requires contact and time. Rare, and made deliberately, either by a Turned lineage or by the Church in its Sentinels.

**Wraith-blade.** A vigil-blade's dark counterpart, made by quenching a stolen, unwilling death into the steel. Burns hot and fast and must be fed killings.

**Wrought, the.** The underworld's name for the Sentinels, marking them as made things.

**Wylter the Eternal.** The Emperor, who built a religion and an empire to hide that he drinks Erdana at the Heart. Deathless for two centuries, he is the valve whose removal would flood the world green and then empty it in a decade.
